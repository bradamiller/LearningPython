In Module 1 the robot only ever did what you told it, step by step. Now it gets to
**sense** the world. This lesson introduces the XRP's reflectance sensors — the
"eyes" it uses to tell a white surface from a black line — and you'll record the
readings you'll rely on for the rest of the module.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain how a reflectance sensor tells light surfaces from dark ones
- Read the left and right sensors with `get_left()` and `get_right()`
- Interpret sensor values (roughly `0.0` on white to `1.0` on black)
- Pick a **threshold** that separates "on the line" from "off the line"
- Record calibration data for your own robot and tape

</Objectives>

## Part 1 · How the robot "sees" a line

Flip an XRP over and you'll find two small reflectance sensors underneath. Each one
shines invisible infrared light down at the surface and measures how much bounces
back.

<Figure
  placeholderLabel="Diagram — IR sensor shining light: white reflects a lot (low value), black absorbs it (high value)"
  caption="A white surface reflects most of the light back (a low reading); a black line absorbs it (a high reading)."
/>

- **White surface** → most light bounces back → a **low** value (around `0.1–0.3`).
- **Black line** → light is absorbed → a **high** value (around `0.7–0.9`).

The XRP has a **left** and a **right** sensor, each with its own reading — which is
how the robot can tell *which side* of a line it's on.

  explanation="High values (near 1.0) mean little light came back — a dark surface absorbs it. White gives low values near 0.0."

## Part 2 · Read the sensors in Python

Reading a sensor is just importing its class and calling a method:

```python
from XRPLib.reflectance import Reflectance
from XRPLib.board import Board

reflectance = Reflectance.get_default_reflectance()
board = Board.get_default_board()

board.wait_for_button()

left = reflectance.get_left()
right = reflectance.get_right()
print("Left sensor:", left)
print("Right sensor:", right)
```

To watch the values change as you slide the robot across the line, put the read in
a loop:

```python
import time

for i in range(50):
    left = reflectance.get_left()
    right = reflectance.get_right()
    print("Left:", left, "  Right:", right)
    time.sleep(0.1)
```

  caption="Slide the robot slowly across the line and watch the readings climb from ~0.2 to ~0.8."
/>

<TeacherNote title="Setup that makes or breaks the readings">

Press the black tape down flat (no bubbles), keep the sensors a few millimeters
off the surface, and watch out for direct sunlight or bright overhead lights —
they shift the values (calibration handles this). Emphasize `print()` as the
number-one debugging tool all module. Have students **save their calibration data**;
they'll reuse it in Lessons 2–7.

</TeacherNote>

## Part 3 · Calibrate and pick a threshold

Every surface and every roll of tape is a little different, so you **calibrate** —
measure your own values — instead of trusting a textbook number. Record readings in
three positions: both sensors on white, both on the black line, and one on the edge.

Then pick a **threshold** halfway between your highest white reading and your lowest
black reading. That's the number your programs will use to decide "on the line" vs.
"off."

> threshold = (highest white value + lowest black value) ÷ 2

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="Your 'off line' (white) values average 0.2 and your 'on line' (black) values average 0.8. What threshold should you choose?"
  options={[

    {text: '0.8'},

  explanation="Halfway between 0.2 and 0.8 is 0.5. A reading above it means the line; below it means white."
/>

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="Why does the XRP have two reflectance sensors instead of one?"
  options={[
    {text: 'As a spare in case one breaks'},
    {text: 'So it can tell which side of the line it is on', correct: true},

<TeacherNote title="Why calibrate instead of just using 0.5?">

0.5 is a fine starting guess, but a glossy surface, a faded line, or bright room
lighting can push the real edge value well off 0.5. Students who calibrate get
line-following that actually works in Lessons 5–6; students who guess spend those
lessons fighting a bad threshold. Frame calibration as the professional habit it
is — real robots calibrate their sensors on startup.

</TeacherNote>

## Wrap-up

- Does a higher reading mean lighter or darker? (Darker — less light bounced back.)
- What is a threshold, and how do you pick one? (The cutoff between line and white; halfway between your calibrated values.)
- Why two sensors? (To tell which side of the line the robot is on.)
