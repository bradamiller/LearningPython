In Module 1 your robot did exactly what it was told: drive 20 cm, turn 90°, repeat.
It never found out where it actually was. Nudge it, or put it on a slippery floor,
and it carries on with the plan anyway and ends up somewhere else.

This module changes that. By the end of it, your robot will **follow a taped circle
on its own, find a short strip of tape across the line, turn around there, and do it
again**, four times, then stop. Nobody tells it how far to drive or how long to turn.
Every stop and every turn ends because the robot *saw* something.

## Sense, think, act

In Lesson 1-0 you decided that a robot has to do three things: **sense** the world,
**think** about what it sensed, and **act**. In Module 1 your XRP mostly acted. It
drove, turned and drew shapes, but it never sensed anything, so every decision was
yours, made in advance.

Module 2 adds the first sense. At the front of the XRP, facing the floor, are two
small **reflectance sensors** that tell a white floor from a strip of black tape. Every
program in this module is built on one question the robot asks over and over, many
times a second: *what do my sensors see right now, and what should I do about it?*

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Module 1" title="Told in advance">
    Drive 20 cm, then turn 90°. The robot finishes the move whatever happens, and only finds out it was wrong when you look at the floor.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Module 2" title="Ended by a sensor">
    Drive until the sensors see the tape. The move ends where the tape is, wherever that turns out to be.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

That second card is the rule for the rest of the course: **the robot stops on
something it senses, not on a clock or a distance.** It's why the same program still
works when the batteries are low or the floor is different.

## One file, all year

Up to now every lesson started a new program. From Lesson 2-1 on you keep **one
file**, `robot.py`, and every lesson adds a little to it. Module 2 builds its first two
**sections**:

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Section 1" title="LINE SENSOR">
    Small functions that <strong>read the tape</strong>: what each sensor sees, and whether the robot is on the line or at a cross.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Section 2" title="LINE TRACKING">
    Functions that <strong>drive on the tape</strong>: follow the line to the next cross, park on it, turn onto the next line. They use the first section and never read the hardware themselves.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

Those two sections drive your robot for the rest of the course. In Module 3 they drive
it around a grid, and in Modules 4 and 5 they drive the routes your robot plans for
itself. You write them once, carefully, and test each function before you build on it.

<TeacherNote title="This is an advance organizer — keep it high-level">

The point of this page is the destination and the shape of the file, not any one
technique. Don't name or explain the constructs the lessons are built to make students
need (the `while` loop in 2-2, `if/else` in 2-3, the error and gain in 2-5): each
lesson lets students hit the limitation first, and a preview here would give that away.
If students ask *how* the robot will follow the line, "that's Lesson 2-5" is the whole
answer. Show the animation, then come back to it at the start of Lesson 2-10.

The "stop on a sensor, not a clock" rule is worth stating out loud: it's the reason the
course never drives on timers from here on, and students who saw the timed program
misbehave on two surfaces in Lesson 1-6 already know why.

</TeacherNote>

## Your roadmap

Each lesson teaches the robot one new thing it can do with what it senses:

| Lesson | What the robot learns to do |
|---|---|
| **2-1 · The Reflectance Sensor** | Read its two sensors, and tell the tape from the floor. `robot.py` is born. |
| **2-2 · Drive to the Edge** | Drive forward and stop the moment it reaches the line. |
| **2-3 · Bounce Driving** | Keep going: turn back every time it meets the line, like a ball in a box. |
| **2-4 · Random Turns** | Turn a different amount each bounce, so it explores instead of repeating itself. |
| **2-5 · Proportional Control** | Actually follow the line, steering a little all the time instead of bouncing. |
| **2-6 · Two-Sensor Following** | Follow the center of the line with both sensors, more smoothly. |
| **2-7 · Sensor Toolkit** | Finish the LINE SENSOR section, and test the whole thing by hand. |
| **2-8 · Intersections** | Notice a cross in the tape, and stop there. |
| **2-9 · Line-Tracking Functions** | Park on the cross and turn onto the next line, ended by the sensors every time. |
| **2-10 · Final Project** | Put it together: lap the circle, reversing at the cross, four times. |

The final project needs almost no new code. By then it is a short list of calls to
functions you wrote and tested earlier, which is the payoff of building the file one
tested piece at a time. (If you take on the optional class extensions in Lessons 2-7
and 2-9, the same project runs on your objects instead.)

## Wrap-up

- What will the robot do by the end of the module? (Follow the circle, reverse at the cross four times, and stop.)
- What ends each move in this module? (Something the sensors see, never a distance or a timer.)
- What is `robot.py`, and what are its first two sections? (The one file you keep all year; LINE SENSOR reads the tape, LINE TRACKING drives on it.)

<TeacherNote title="Materials for the module">

Lesson 2-1 needs only a strip of black electrical tape on a white surface (the
whiteboard panels work well). From Lesson 2-2 on, students work inside a taped circle,
and from Lesson 2-7 the circle needs the short strip across it that makes the cross.
Students record calibration readings in Lesson 2-1 that they use all module, so have
them keep the numbers somewhere they won't lose.

</TeacherNote>
