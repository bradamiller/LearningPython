A room running this course will have students who have never typed a line of code
next to students who have written games. The course is built to stretch across
that range without separate lessons for each group, and most of the tools for it
are already on the lesson pages. This page is how to use them, plus general
strategies that work in any programming class. Many lessons also have a teacher
note on differentiation with ideas specific to that lesson.

## Find out where students start

Before Module 1, a short survey or a first-day conversation tells you most of what
you need:

1. Have they programmed before? In what?
2. How comfortable are they with coordinates, angles and distance?
3. Do they jump in or plan first?
4. Do they prefer to work alone, in pairs or in groups?

Lesson 1-0's discussion is also a good first read of a class: who reasons out loud,
who waits, who argues with a neighbor.

## What the course already gives you

**Activities come in steps, each tested before the next.** From Module 2 on, a
lesson builds one function at a time and tests it on the robot before moving on.
For a student who is struggling, getting one step solid is a real result; don't
let them skip ahead to the full program.

**Skeletons, not blank files.** Each lesson hands students an outline of the code to
write: the function names, and a comment per step in the early modules. The amount
of help fades as the course goes on, and fades more for simple functions than for
hard ones. For a student who needs more, write a few more of the comment steps
with them; for one who needs less, have them write their own outline before
looking at the lesson's.

**Check your work boxes.** Closed boxes on the page hold worked results for
students to compare against *after* trying. They help a stuck student check their
thinking without handing them the answer to work they turn in. Encourage "try,
then check," and watch for students who open them first.

**Optional extensions for students who finish early.** From Lesson 2-7 on, some
lessons end with a class version of the code the student has just written as
functions. They are gated on working code, not on ability, so a student can reach
one in one lesson and miss the next. They earn no points, which keeps them a
stretch rather than a requirement.

**Templates to catch up.** The [robot.py templates](/templates) page has every
student's file, with outlines and no answers, as it stands after each lesson. A
student who was absent, or whose file is beyond repair, takes the file for the
lesson before and carries on with the class.

**Testing without the robot.** From Module 4 on, planners are tested on screen
before they drive. A student who is waiting for a robot, or who learns better by
tracing than by watching, can do real work at a computer.

**Cut points.** If a whole class is behind, the course can be shortened without
leaving it unfinished. See
[Planning the semester](/teacher-guide/planning#if-the-class-is-behind).

## Students new to programming

- **Don't rush Blockly.** Module 1's Blockly lessons are where sequencing, loops and
  functions make sense before syntax can get in the way. An extra week there pays
  back in Python.
- **Bridge to Python side by side.** When Python starts in 1-8, put a Blockly
  program next to its Python version and read them line by line together.
- **Read code aloud.** In guided practice, read each line and say what it does.
- **Pair program.** One student types, one reads and checks, and they swap often.
- **Check in constantly and briefly.** "What is your code doing right now? What did
  you expect?" is usually enough to get a student moving again.
- **In Module 2,** get one-sensor following solid before two-sensor following, and
  keep the calibration numbers handy; many "my code is broken" moments are a
  threshold from the wrong surface.

## Students who are behind

**Make the task smaller.** Break "write the polygon function" into "write the
loop," then "add the turn," then "make the number of sides a parameter." A checklist
of three steps is easier to start than one big instruction.

**Show it more than one way.**

- Plain-English steps before Python
- A flowchart of the loop on the whiteboard
- The algorithm walked on the floor: a student walks the grid, calling out
  intersections, before the robot does
- Worked examples: "a triangle has 3 sides and turns 120°; a square…?"

**Debug as a method, not a mood.** Teach three questions and keep asking them: what
did you expect to happen, what actually happened, and where do they first differ? A
`print()` is the tool that answers the last one; the course leans on it from
Module 2 on.

**Keep the scope, change the size.** A Module 4 project with two destinations
instead of four, or a capstone with one obstacle, still shows every idea.

**Look after the student, too.** Debugging is frustrating for everyone. Say so,
share your own bugs, celebrate the run that finally works, and use "you haven't got
it yet" rather than "you can't." Start with tasks a student can finish, and raise
the difficulty as they succeed.

## Students who are ahead

- **Move faster through Blockly**, but keep them in it long enough to get a feel for
  the robot: how it drives, how it turns, what a parameter changes.
- **Open-ended challenges** in Module 1: a star, a spiral, a nest of polygons, a
  drawing of their own.
- **The optional extensions**, from Lesson 2-7 on, and Module 6.
- **The challenges at the end of the projects:** the short way round in 4-8, a
  return-home leg, visiting the nearest destination next, re-checking old obstacles
  after a few runs.
- **Bigger questions:** how is A* different from Dijkstra? Could two robots share
  the grid without colliding? What would you need to make the robot faster rather
  than shorter?
- **Leadership:** reviewing classmates' code, helping debug, writing up a tricky bug
  for the class.

## Grouping

- **Pairs that complement each other**, like a strong coder with a strong builder,
  work well. Rotate partners so nobody settles into one role.
- **Group by need for short workshops:** sensor tuning, the turn, the planner's
  loop.
- **Mix levels for projects**, where a range of strengths makes a better robot.
- **One robot per pair works; one each is better from Module 2 on,** where line
  tracking is fiddly and students need their own runs.

## Accommodations

Students' plans come first; these are ideas that fit this course.

**Vision.** Enlarge the editor and the projector text, and use a high-contrast
theme. Describe figures and animations aloud. The lesson figures carry alt text for
screen readers.

**Hearing.** Give instructions in writing as well as aloud; the lesson pages already
are the written version, so point to them.

**Motor and dexterity.** Pair the student with a partner for handling the robot, and
let them lead the code. Testing on screen, from Module 4 on, needs no robot at all.

**Attention.** Shorter work blocks with a clear goal each, visual checklists of the
steps, and frequent check-ins. The test-each-function-before-the-next structure
already gives natural break points.

**Autism.** Explicit instructions, predictable routines (the lesson structure is the
same every day), a quieter place to test when the room is loud, and advance notice
of changes.

**More time.** Allow extra time for debugging and polishing projects, and allow
resubmission with feedback.

## Different ways to show mastery

- Code that runs and meets the requirements
- A walk-through: "tell me what this does"
- A demonstration of the robot, with the design explained
- A portfolio of tests and fixes across a module
- A written or spoken reflection on what they learned

## A classroom culture that helps

- **Normalize bugs.** Every programmer spends much of their time debugging. Show
  yours.
- **Ask three before me.** Classmates first, then the teacher.
- **Praise strategy and persistence,** not talent.
- **Different solutions are fine.** The "Check your work" boxes say so: a version
  that works differently from the one shown is still right.
