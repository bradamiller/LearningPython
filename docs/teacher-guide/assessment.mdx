Students show what they've learned by **making a robot do something and explaining
how**, not by taking tests. Assessment in this course rests on four kinds of
evidence:

1. Code that runs on the robot and does what the activity asked
2. The tests they wrote and ran along the way
3. How they debugged: what went wrong, and how they found it
4. Their own explanation of the code and the choices in it

The last one matters most. Teacher mode keeps solutions off students' screens, but
the site is static and a determined student can find them. A student who can walk
you through their own code, line by line, wrote it or understands it, and both are
worth credit.

## Formative: every lesson

**Activities.** Each lesson's hand-in work is headed `Activity · …` on the page, so
there's no guessing what gets submitted. Grade them for completion, or with a
three-level mark: done, in progress, not started. The point is to see who is stuck
before the module project, not to rank.

**Knowledge checks.** The interactive checks give students instant feedback while
they read. For evidence you can collect, print the lesson's sheet from
[Printable Checks](/checks): students circle an answer and write a line on why,
and the answer key is linked from each lesson in teacher mode. The "why" line is
where misconceptions show.

**Exit tickets.** Most lessons' last teacher note suggests one: a single question
that tells you whether the main idea landed.

**The tests in `robot.py`.** From Module 2 on, every function is tested in the
lesson that writes it, and the test functions stay in the file. By the end of a
module a student's file is a record of everything their robot has learned to do,
and of how they checked it. Skim it, and ask them to run a test or two in front of
you.

**Debugging logs.** A running list, kept by the student, of errors they hit and how
they fixed them. Review it weekly. It rewards the habit the course is built on, and
it shows you which bugs a whole class keeps meeting.

**Code reviews.** After a big activity, pairs swap code with a short checklist:
does it run, can I read it, what would I change? Reviewing someone else's code
is a skill in itself, and it spreads good habits faster than you can.

**Observation.** Who asks questions, who helps a neighbor, who keeps going after
the third failed run. Persistence is most of programming a robot.

## Summative: the module projects

Each module ends with a project that pulls the module together. The rubrics below
are starting points; adjust the weights to your class. Where a lesson page already
shows students a rubric (Modules 4 and 5), the table here matches it.

### Module 1 · Final project (Lesson 1-12)

Students choose a project: a polygon art piece, a precision delivery route, a
sensor behavior as a stretch, or their own idea cleared with you. It runs as a
multi-day arc: plan, build, test, document, present. Have teams turn in a short
written plan before they code. 50 points, weighted about equally:

| Category | Points | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Design and planning | 10 | A written plan before coding: the problem, a function list, pseudocode |
| Implementation | 10 | Loops, functions and parameters used where they belong; runs without errors |
| Testing | 10 | Evidence each piece was tested on its own before the whole program |
| Documentation | 10 | Comments and docstrings a classmate could follow |
| Reflection and presentation | 10 | A short demo, and answers to: what did you build, what concepts did you use and why, what was the hardest bug, what would you add |

Reward process and debugging, not just a flawless demo. Lesson 1-7's Blockly
challenges make a good earlier checkpoint: a precision course finished within
±30 cm, and a creative challenge with a short written reflection.

### Module 2 · Final project (Lesson 2-10)

The robot follows the taped circle, detects the cross, reverses, repeats for four
reversals, then stops and prints a message, using the LINE SENSOR and LINE TRACKING
sections of `robot.py`. 50 points:

| Category | Points | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Code organization | 15 | A short project function made mostly of calls to their own functions; earlier sections reused, not rewritten |
| Functionality | 15 | Tracks the circle smoothly, detects the cross, and completes four reversals |
| Testing and debugging | 10 | Each function's test kept and passing; tuning recorded |
| Presentation | 10 | Demonstrates the run and explains the design |

Students who built the `LineSensor` and `LineTrack` classes should build this
project on them. That earns no extra points: see the note on extensions below.

### Module 3 · Final project (Lesson 3-4)

The robot drives a square on the grid, `SIDE` intersections per side, turning at
each corner, and ends where it started. 50 points:

| Category | Points | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Reuse | 10 | Uses the earlier sections of `robot.py` unchanged |
| A loop | 10 | One drive-and-turn sequence repeated, not four copies |
| Four sides | 10 | Each side drives the right number of intersections and turns onto the next line |
| Back to the start | 10 | The square closes: the robot ends on the intersection it started from |
| Progress prints | 10 | Prints that show where the robot thinks it is at each corner |

Reward the tuning process; a square that closes on the third try, with the reason
written down, is a good result.

### Module 4 · Final project (Lesson 4-9)

The robot visits four or more destinations: for each leg it computes the path,
prints it, waits for the button, and drives it. Tested in three levels: on screen,
then one leg on the robot, then the full run. 50 points, as shown to students on
the lesson page:

| Category | Points | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| Planner | 10 | `compute_manhattan_path()` correct for any position and destination; the Lesson 4-6 tests pass |
| Driving functions | 15 | Correct `desired_heading()`, `turn_to()` and `drive_path()`; parked on the cross at every arrival; position and heading returned |
| Main program | 10 | Four or more destinations; prints the first leg before the button; drives each leg; keeps `position` and `heading` up to date from `drive_path()` |
| Robot demo | 10 | Physically visits every destination with correct turns |
| Planning and documentation | 5 | Planning worksheet, a hand-trace of one leg, the testing checklist followed |

### Module 5 · Capstone (Lesson 5-9)

The course's capstone. The robot visits a list of destinations on a grid with
obstacles, detects blocked intersections with the rangefinder, re-plans around
them, prints what it discovered, and on a second run starts from that list. 60
points, as shown to students on the lesson page:

| Category | Points | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| BFS pathfinding | 10 | Correct shortest paths; the Lesson 5-6 tests pass |
| Driving integration | 10 | Drives the computed paths, leg after leg |
| Obstacle detection | 10 | The rangefinder detects a blocked intersection and `add_blocked()` records it |
| Re-planning | 10 | Reroutes correctly around what it discovered |
| Experience | 10 | Run 1 prints what it found; Run 2 starts from that list |
| Demonstration | 5 | Run 1 discovers and reroutes; Run 2 avoids the known obstacles |
| Code quality | 5 | Readable, built from functions, meaningful comments |

Insist on the dry-run version, with the rangefinder simulated, before a box touches
the grid. A wrong reroute in simulation is a logic bug and nothing else.

### Module 6 (optional)

Module 6 has no graded project. Assess it formatively: the two-run table from 6-4,
with costs and times that agree, and an explanation of why the route with more
steps won.

## The capstone demo

Run the Module 5 demo live, one team at a time. You place the obstacles; the team
runs twice and reports its numbers: "Run 1 took X steps with Y reroutes; Run 2
took A steps with B reroutes." Then ask *why* Run 2 was better, and listen for
"better data," not "the robot got smarter."

- **Excellent:** both runs work; the second is measurably better; the student
  explains the planner, the detection and the reason for the improvement clearly
- **Good:** both runs work with minor hiccups; solid explanation
- **Satisfactory:** most of it works with some live debugging; a basic explanation
- **Needs work:** major parts don't work, or the student can't explain how their
  code behaves

Follow it with a short written reflection: what went well, what was hard, what
they learned. For 50-minute periods the capstone usually spans two or three days:
assembly and simulation, robot testing, then demos.

## Optional extensions earn no points

The class versions of student code, from Lesson 2-7 on, are optional and gated on
working functions. Give them no points, or at most a small challenge credit. If
"code organization" on a rubric quietly rewards the class version, the extension
becomes a requirement with extra steps, and the students it was meant to protect
are the ones who lose marks. A student who built a class should use it from then
on, and is graded on exactly the same rubric. The reasoning is on the
[course design](/teacher-guide/course-design#d1-functions-are-the-spine-classes-are-a-per-student-extension)
page.

## Rubrics for any module

### Code quality

| | Excellent | Good | Satisfactory | Needs work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Works** | Runs and meets every requirement | Runs; a minor bug or a missing feature | Mostly works; several bugs | Doesn't run, or incomplete |
| **Readable** | Clear structure and names; settings in capitals | Mostly clear | Hard to follow in places | Hard to follow throughout |
| **Comments** | Explain the why, not just the what | Explain the key logic | Some, not always helpful | Few or none |
| **Tested** | Each function tested on its own; tests kept in the file | Tested in the main cases | Minimal testing | Not tested |
| **Debugging** | Found and fixed their own bugs, and can say how | Fixed bugs with a little help | Needed help to debug | Couldn't debug |

### Algorithms (Modules 4 to 6)

| | Excellent | Good | Satisfactory | Needs work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Logic** | Correct, including edge cases (already there, blocked, no path) | Correct; misses an edge case | Basic version; some errors | Significant misunderstanding |
| **Tested on screen** | Planner tested with many cases before the robot drove | Tested on the standard cases | Minimal testing | Went straight to the robot |
| **Explanation** | Explains the algorithm and why it's correct | Explains the algorithm | Explains the basic idea | Can't explain it |
| **Separation** | The planner only plans: it reads no sensors and drives nothing | Mostly separate | Partly tangled with driving | Planning and driving mixed together |

## Grading philosophy

**Grade what students can do by the end, not the average of their attempts.**
Early work is formative, feedback-first and low-stakes. Projects are graded
against the rubric. Allow resubmission when a student has used the feedback.

A starting point for weights, to adjust to your setting:

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Formative: activities, checks, reviews, logs | 40% |
| Module projects (Modules 1 to 4) | 40% |
| Capstone demo and reflection | 20% |

To pass, a student should complete every module project, at least in part,
present at the capstone demo, and show growth across the semester.

## Feedback

- **Activities:** fast and short. Does it run, does it do what was asked? Same day
  or next where you can.
- **Projects:** a score and a comment on each rubric row, plus a short conversation
  about one strength and one next step. Return it before the next module's project
  starts.
- **Capstone:** celebrate during the demo; written feedback and the grade within a
  week.

When you grade, keep asking: can the student explain what their code does? Did they
debug, or give up? Do they understand the idea, or only the syntax? Has their
problem-solving improved since Module 1?
