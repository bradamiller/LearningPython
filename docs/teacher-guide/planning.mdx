Modules 1 to 5 fill a semester of about **18 weeks at three 50-minute periods a
week**. Module 6 is optional and adds about two more. The [pacing guide](/pacing)
has the lesson-by-lesson times, totals for your own period length and meetings per
week, and printable planning sheets; it is generated from the lessons, so trust it
over the rounded numbers on this page.

## The semester at a glance

At three 50-minute periods a week, counting each multi-day project as two periods:

| Weeks | Module | Lessons | What students build |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 5 | **1 · Learning to Drive** | 1-0 to 1-12 | Blockly programs that drive and draw shapes, then the same ideas in Python: loops, functions, parameters, and a project of their own |
| 6 to 9 | **2 · Line Tracking** | 2-0 to 2-10 | `robot.py` is born: sensor functions, a proportional line follower, intersection detection, and a program that laps a taped circle |
| 10 to 11 | **3 · Grid Driving** | 3-0 to 3-4 | Driving intersection to intersection, turning on the grid, and a square that closes |
| 11 to 14 | **4 · Manhattan Navigation** | 4-0 to 4-9 | Coordinates, tuples and lists; a route planner tested on screen; a navigator that drives any route |
| 15 to 18 | **5 · Pathfinding Around Obstacles** | 5-0 to 5-9 | Breadth-first search, the planner swap, rangefinder obstacle detection, re-planning, and the capstone |
| (19 to 20) | **6 · Dijkstra's Algorithm** (optional) | 6-0 to 6-5 | A grid with one slow segment, and a planner that plans by time |

Module 2 is the heaviest module for most classes. Module 3 is the lightest, and the
one to compress if you are short on time. Modules 3 to 5 build one continuous
system on the same grid, so run them without a long break between them.

## Milestones

What students should have working by the end of each module. If most of a class
is short of one of these, slow down there; every module after it builds on it.

**Module 1 · Learning to Drive**

- The robot drives and turns reliably, and students can explain why it waits for
  the button (1-1)
- A square from a repeat block, and the 360 ÷ sides rule for any regular polygon
  (1-2)
- A `polygon(sides, side_length)` function in Blockly, tested with several shapes
  (1-5)
- The difference between blocks that finish (`Straight`, `Turn`) and blocks that
  only start the motors (`Set effort`, `Arcade`) (1-6)
- The same shapes written in Python with `for` loops and functions (1-10, 1-11)
- A finished, presented Module 1 project (1-12)

**Module 2 · Line Tracking**

- Calibration data and a threshold for their own robot and tape (2-1)
- A robot that drives to the line and stops on a sensor, not a timer (2-2)
- Bounce driving inside the circle, then with random turns (2-3, 2-4)
- A tuned proportional line follower, one sensor then two (2-5, 2-6)
- The sensor toolkit tested function by function, and intersections detected
  (2-7, 2-8)
- The line-tracking functions, then the final project: four reversals on the
  circle and a clean stop (2-9, 2-10)

**Module 3 · Grid Driving**

- Reach the next intersection and park on it (3-1)
- Drive a given number of intersections with one call (3-2)
- Turn on the grid, sensor-ended, and drive an L (3-3)
- A square of `SIDE` intersections that ends where it started (3-4)

**Module 4 · Manhattan Navigation**

- Problem decomposition: pairs break "drive to a destination" into sub-problems, then a
  four-step method: the whole problem, the parts and what passes between them (a list of
  tuples), each part built and tested alone, then connected (4-0, with a
  callout at 4-4, 4-7 and 4-8)
- Coordinates on the grid, and tuples and lists in Python (4-1 to 4-3)
- The Manhattan algorithm traced on paper, then coded as
  `compute_manhattan_path()` (4-4, 4-5)
- A suite of tests that runs with no robot, and a failing test debugged (4-6)
- `desired_heading()`, `turn_to()` and `drive_path()`, checked on paper and then
  on the robot (4-7, 4-8)
- The final project: four or more destinations, each planned, printed and driven
  (4-9)

**Module 5 · Pathfinding Around Obstacles**

- The grid as a graph, built with a dictionary (5-1, 5-2)
- Breadth-first search traced by hand, then implemented as `compute_bfs_path()`
  (5-3 to 5-5)
- A test harness that checks what must be true of a path, run against both
  planners (5-6)
- Obstacles detected with the rangefinder and routed around (5-7)
- A second run that starts from what the first run learned (5-8)
- The capstone demo: discover, re-plan, and improve on the second run (5-9)

**Module 6 · Dijkstra's Algorithm (optional)**

- A slow segment timed with a stopwatch and turned into a weight (6-1, 6-2)
- BFS copied and changed in two places into Dijkstra (6-3)
- Two runs on the slow grid, BFS against Dijkstra, with the times to compare (6-4)

## Checkpoints

The module projects are the natural places to stop, assess and regroup:

| Checkpoint | Lesson | About week | What's handed in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Module 1 | 1-12 | 5 | A Python project of the student's choice, planned, built, tested and presented |
| Module 2 | 2-10 | 9 | Four reversals on the taped circle, built from the sensor and line-tracking sections |
| Module 3 | 3-4 | 11 | A square on the grid that closes |
| Module 4 | 4-9 | 14 | Navigation to four or more destinations |
| Capstone | 5-9 | 18 | Navigation around discovered obstacles, and a better second run |

The [assessment page](/teacher-guide/assessment) has the rubrics for each one.

Module 1 also has two natural pauses of its own, marked in its lesson headers: after
the Blockly foundation (1-5), and after the driving challenges (1-7), before
Python starts in 1-8.

## A typical lesson

Most lessons are one 50 to 60 minute period, and most run the same way: a short
look at the problem, a try on the robot, the idea named once students have felt
the need for it, then the activity. That order matters in this course. Lessons are
written so students meet the problem before the construct that solves it, and a
lesson taught explanation-first loses the point. The teacher notes on each page
say where the timing usually runs long.

## If the class is ahead

- Point students at the optional extensions: the class versions of their code, from
  Lesson 2-7 on. They are gated on working code, so they suit a student who
  finishes early.
- Use the stretch ideas at the end of each project: a return-home leg, a round trip,
  visiting the nearest destination next, a map printed after each leg.
- Run Module 6.

## If the class is behind

- Don't skip Module 1's Blockly lessons. They build the intuition the Python
  lessons rely on, and they move fast once students have it.
- Give Module 2 an extra week before you cut anything else.
- Compress Module 3. It is four short lessons that establish the grid.
- Use the [robot.py templates](/templates) so a student who fell behind starts the
  next lesson with the right file rather than a broken one.
- Shrink project scope rather than skipping projects: two destinations instead of
  four, one obstacle instead of several.
- Use the course's cut points, described on
  [How this course works](/how-this-course-works#the-course-can-be-made-shorter):
  stop after Manhattan, teach pathfinding without discovering obstacles while
  driving, or leave out Module 6.
