Every `Straight` and `Turn` you've used is really controlling two motors behind
the scenes. In this lesson you'll take the wheel yourself — driving the left and
right motors **directly** to make curves, spins, and smooth steering that the
high-level blocks can't do on their own. Along the way you'll find out what the
word **effort** actually means, and why it isn't the same thing as speed.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain **differential drive** — steering by running the two wheels at different speeds
- Predict the robot's motion from what each side's motor is doing
- Use **Set effort** to control the motors directly (`-1` to `1`), and explain how **effort** differs from **speed**
- Steer with **Arcade**, and say what changing its **Turn** number does to the size of the circle the robot drives
- Decide when to use direct motor control vs. the high-level `Straight`/`Turn`

</Objectives>

## How the robot really steers

The XRP is a **differential drive** robot: it has no steering wheel. The only
thing it can control is **how fast each side turns, and in which direction** —
and that turns out to be enough to produce every motion it can make.

### The whole rule, in three lines

<Reveal title="What each case does" hint="after all four predictions">

1. **Both sides forward, matched** — drives straight ahead.
2. **Both forward, one faster** — the faster side travels farther in the same
   time, so the robot arcs *toward the slower side*.
3. **One side stopped** — the stopped wheel stays put and the driven side walks
   around it: the robot swings about that wheel.
4. **Opposite directions** — one side pushes forward while the other pulls back,
   so the robot spins on the spot.

</Reveal>

Every one of those outcomes comes from the same idea — you operate the left and
right sides of the driveline independently:

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Same" title="Both sides, same speed">
    Drives straight. Match the two sides and there's nothing to turn the robot.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Different" title="One side faster">
    Turns. The bigger the difference, the tighter the curve — until one side stops and it swings about that wheel.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Opposite" title="Each side opposite">
    Turns in place. The robot rotates about its own center without going anywhere.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

### Commanding the motors: the Set effort block

The **Set effort** block gives you that direct control. Effort runs from `-1`
(full reverse) through `0` (stopped) to `1` (full forward), and you set each wheel
separately — so each of the four cases above is just a pair of effort numbers.

There's a catch, and it's the big idea of this lesson. `Straight` **finishes**:
it drives its 20 cm, stops the motors, and hands the program to the next block.
`Set effort` doesn't finish — it switches the motors on and moves straight to the
next block, leaving them running. They keep running until something stops them.

So a `Set effort` program has to answer a question `Straight` never asks you:
**when should the motors stop?** For a first look, we'll answer it with a
**Sleep** — but keep an eye on how well that works.

### Effort is not the same as speed

The block is called Set **effort**, not "set speed," and the difference matters.
Effort is **how hard the motors try** — electrically, it's how much voltage they
get. Think of it the way you'd think about your own effort in a sport:

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-06/motor-effort-analogy.jpg"
  alt="Line drawings of a cyclist, a skater, a swimmer, and a hiker, illustrating effort"
  caption="Effort is how hard you're working — not how fast you're actually going."
/>

Those two things come apart as soon as the world pushes back. Pedal a bike uphill
at a *constant* effort and you slow down; the same effort on the flat carries you
much faster. **Effort** is your input. **Speed** is the result — and it depends on
the hill, the carpet, the battery, and how much the robot is carrying.

**Speed** is the other way of asking: you name the speed you want, and the robot
*adjusts its own effort* to hold it — pushing harder up the hill, easing off on
the way down:

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-06/effort-vs-speed.jpg"
  alt="Diagram of a robot climbing and descending a hill, using big effort uphill, less effort at the top, and little effort downhill"
  caption="Ask for a speed and the effort changes to maintain it: big effort uphill, little effort coming down."
/>

This is why the same `Set effort` numbers don't always produce the same motion.
Effort `0.5` on a smooth floor is quicker than effort `0.5` on thick carpet, and
quicker again with a fresh battery. It's also why `Straight` and `Turn` are more
repeatable than raw effort — they watch the robot's sensors and adjust, instead
of blindly holding one voltage.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="You set both motors to effort 0.5 and drive across a smooth floor, then across a thick carpet. What happens?"
  options={[
    {text: 'The robot travels at the same speed on both — effort sets the speed'},

    {text: 'It goes faster on the carpet'},
    {text: 'The robot refuses to move on carpet'},
  ]}
  explanation="Effort is the input (roughly, motor voltage), not the outcome. The same effort against more resistance gives less speed — exactly like pedalling a bike uphill."
/>

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="A robot is asked to hold a steady speed as it drives up and over a hill. What does its effort do?"
  options={[

    {text: 'Rises going uphill and drops going downhill, to keep the speed the same', correct: true},
    {text: 'Drops going uphill'},

  explanation="That's the trade: commanding speed means the robot changes effort by itself to maintain what you asked for. Commanding effort means you fix the effort and let the speed vary."

<TeacherNote title="Effort vs. speed — worth naming explicitly">

Students will otherwise read "effort 0.5" as "half speed," and then be puzzled
when the same program behaves differently on carpet, on a ramp, or with a tired
battery. The bike-uphill analogy lands fastest: same effort, different speed. If
you have a ramp or a carpet square handy, run one `Set effort` program on both
surfaces back to back — thirty seconds of demo and the distinction sticks.

This is also the honest answer to "why do we even have `Straight` and `Turn`?"
Those close the loop with sensors to deliver a requested distance or angle;
`Set effort` is open-loop and gives you whatever the world allows. Module 2's
line-following builds directly on this idea of adjusting effort based on what the
robot senses.

</TeacherNote>

## Blocks that finish vs. blocks that don't

Run the program above three times: once with a fresh battery, once on carpet, and
once on a smooth floor. Measure how far the robot goes each time.

Three seconds at effort `0.5` is not a distance. It's three seconds — and the
distance you get out of it changes with the battery, the surface, the weight on
the robot, even whether the floor slopes. That's the effort-isn't-speed idea from
the first section, showing up as a practical problem.

Now compare the two kinds of block:

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Finishes" title="Straight / Turn">
    Runs until the job is done — the distance driven, the angle turned — then stops the motors by itself. The next block doesn't start until it's finished.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Doesn't finish" title="Set effort / Arcade">
    Switches the motors on and moves to the next block immediately. The motors stay on until something stops them.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

Three things can stop them: a **Stop motors** block <Block name="stop_motors" />,
another `Set effort` (including effort `0`), or the program ending. You don't
need a stop block at the *end* of a program — the robot stops the motors when the
program is over — but any time the motors should quit while the program keeps
going, that's what **Stop motors** is for.

Which leaves the real question: **what should the robot wait for before it
stops?** A Sleep is the crudest possible answer, and you just measured why. The
answer you'll use for the rest of this course is to stop on something the robot
**senses** — drive until the rangefinder says the wall is 10 cm away, until the
reflectance sensor sees the line, until the encoder has counted far enough. Same
`Set effort`, but the robot decides when it's done instead of the clock. That's
where Module 2 goes next.

    {text: 'Straight leaves the motors running; Set effort stops them when it finishes'},
    {text: 'Set effort can only drive backward'},

  explanation="Straight and Turn finish a job and stop. Set effort just sets the motors going — deciding when they stop is now your problem."

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="Why is 'Set effort 0.5, Sleep 3 seconds' a poor way to travel a set distance?"
  options={[
    {text: 'Sleep blocks are slower than Straight blocks'},
    {text: 'Effort is not speed — the same 3 seconds covers different distances depending on battery, surface, and load', correct: true},
    {text: 'Sleep stops the motors, so the robot never gets anywhere'},
    {text: 'The robot cannot run Set effort and Sleep in the same program'},

/>

<TeacherNote title="Straight vs. Set effort — the trade-off">

This is the lesson's big idea: **abstraction vs. control**. `Straight`/`Turn` use
the robot's sensors (encoders/IMU) for precise, repeatable distances and angles —
easy, but limited to straight lines and pivots. `Set effort` is raw and
time-based: less precise, but it can make smooth curves and creative motion the
high-level blocks can't. Neither is "better" — it's about the right tool for the
task. The distinction to drill is **finishing vs. not finishing**: `Straight` and
`Turn` stop the motors themselves when the job is done; `Set effort` and `Arcade`
just start them. Two habits to head off while you're here. First, don't let timed
driving take root — the Sleep in these examples is a placeholder because we have
no sensors yet, and it's worth having students measure the same program on two
surfaces so they see the distance change. Real programs stop on what the robot
senses (Module 2), or use `Straight`/`Turn` when a measured distance is what they
want. Second, nobody needs a stop block at the end of a program — the robot stops
the motors when the program ends; `Stop motors` is for stopping mid-program.

</TeacherNote>

## Smooth steering with Arcade

Setting two efforts by hand is fiddly. The **Arcade** block is friendlier — you
give it two numbers, like a game controller, and it works out the two motor efforts
for you:

- **Straight** — how fast it drives forward (this is the one people call *speed*).
- **Turn** — how hard it bends, and which way. `0` is dead straight; positive and
  negative bend opposite ways.

<TeacherNote title="Differentiation">

**More support:** give a table of effort pairs and their outcomes, and start with
symmetric efforts (0.3/0.3) before curves; let students use Arcade first — it's
more intuitive. **More challenge:** build a spiral by gradually increasing one
side's effort across several Set effort blocks.

</TeacherNote>

## Activity · Get a feel for circles

Before you try to draw a shape with curves, you need a feel for what the two
numbers in **Arcade** actually do to the robot. The fastest way to get it is to
drive circles and watch them change size.

Put a dry-erase marker in the robot's marker holder and work on a whiteboard
panel, and the robot draws its own circle for you to measure. No marker? Chalk
a starting mark on the floor and eyeball it.

### Step 1 · Change one number and watch

Use the three-block program from the section above — wait for button, Arcade,
Sleep — with **Straight** at `0.5` and a Sleep long enough to come round at least
once. Now run it three or four times, changing **only Turn** each time:

| Straight | Turn | How big was the circle? |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.15 | |
| 0.5 | 0.3 | |
| 0.5 | 0.5 | |
| 0.5 | 0.8 | |

You don't need exact measurements here. You're after the *relationship* — one
sentence you could say out loud about what happens to the circle when the turn
number goes up. Write it down before you move on.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="You run Arcade with Straight 0.5 and Turn 0.3, then change Turn to 0.6. What happens to the circle?"
  options={[
    {text: 'It gets bigger'},
    {text: 'It gets smaller — more turn means a tighter circle', correct: true},
    {text: 'It stays the same size but the robot goes faster'},
    {text: 'The robot drives straight'},
  ]}
  explanation="Turn is how hard the robot is bending. More bend for the same forward speed means a tighter circle. Turn 0 is no bend at all — a straight line."
/>

### Step 2 · Find a circle that fits

Now aim for something specific: a circle roughly **12 to 18 inches** (30–45 cm)
across — about the width of a sheet of paper turned sideways, up to a bit more.
That size fits on a whiteboard panel and it will fit on your desk space later.

Don't calculate it. Change Turn, run it, look, change it again. Three or four
tries is normal.

When you've got one you like, **write both numbers down and don't lose them**:

> My circle: Straight ______ Turn ______

Every robot lands on slightly different numbers, and the rest of this lesson
uses yours.

### Step 3 · Time a half circle

Here's the number you actually need. Keep Straight and Turn exactly as they are,
and change only the **Sleep** until the robot comes round to face the **opposite**
direction from where it started — a half circle.

Half of one is easier to judge than a whole one: "is it pointing backwards?" is a
question you can answer by looking, while "is it back where it started?" is a
guess. Start from a wall or a tape line so you have something to judge against.

> My half circle takes ______ seconds

A full circle takes twice that, which is the last thing you need for the next
activity.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="Your half circle takes 1.4 seconds. Roughly how long is a full circle at the same Straight and Turn?"
  options={[
    {text: 'Still 1.4 seconds'},
    {text: 'About 2.8 seconds — twice as far around', correct: true},
    {text: 'About 0.7 seconds'},
    {text: 'There is no way to tell'},
  ]}
  explanation="Nothing about the robot's motion changed — same Straight, same Turn — so going twice as far around the circle takes twice as long."
/>

<TeacherNote title="Why circles first">

This activity exists because students who go straight to Earth – Moon – Earth
spend the period fighting two problems at once: what the numbers do, *and* flying
a four-part mission with them. Split it and both get easier.

Step 1 is genuinely open. Resist handing over "more turn = tighter circle" — it
takes about four runs to see, and a student who says it themselves owns it. The
knowledge check afterwards is the safety net.

**Sizes to expect:** at Straight `0.5`, a Turn somewhere around `0.4`–`0.6` usually
lands in the 12–18 inch range, but don't publish that — it varies with battery,
floor and tyre wear, and the hunt is the point. If a group is stuck, ask which
direction they should move the number rather than which number to use.

**On the markers:** the drawn circle is worth the mess. Students measure it,
compare it with the group next to them, and discover for themselves that
identical numbers give different circles — which is the timed-driving problem
arriving on its own, a full lesson before Module 2 names it.

Step 3's "point the opposite way" test is the one to insist on. Students who try
to judge a full circle will be out by a quarter turn and won't know it, and in the
next activity that error is what makes them miss the Earth.

</TeacherNote>

## Activity · Earth – Moon – Earth

Tape an **Earth** and a **Moon** to your whiteboard panel, about 18–24 inches
(45–60 cm) apart. Your robot is a spacecraft, and its mission is to swing around
the Moon, cross to the Earth, swing around the Earth the other way, and come back
to rejoin its own lunar orbit.

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-06/earth-moon-path.svg"
  alt="An Earth disc and a Moon disc of equal size side by side, making an infinity symbol: a dashed three-quarter circle around each body, joined by two solid straight diagonals that cross at forty-five degrees in the middle. Dots mark the four points where a curve becomes a straight line."
  caption="All four moves. Dashed is curved and timed; solid is straight and measured; the dots are where one becomes the other. The robot starts and finishes at the crossing."
/>

There's a **printable Earth and Moon** in this lesson's Resources at the bottom of
the page — print it, cut both discs out, tape them down.

The two bodies are drawn the same size, which is not remotely true and is very
convenient: it means both of your orbits are the same size, so the numbers you
found in the last activity work for each of them. (The real Moon is about a
quarter of Earth's width, and thirty Earths would fit in the gap between them.)

### Step 1 · Plan the mission on paper

Before you touch a block, draw the path. Mark where the robot starts, which way it
goes round the Moon, where it crosses to the Earth, and which way it goes round
*that*. You should end up with a shape you recognise.

The thing to notice on your drawing: **the robot bends one way around the Moon and
the other way around the Earth.** That's the whole trick, and it's why `Straight`
and `Turn` blocks can't draw this shape at all.

<Callout kind="tip" title="How far apart should the bodies go?">

About **one and a half times your circle's diameter**. Any closer and the crossings
are too shallow to see; any further and they get steep and the path stops looking
like an infinity symbol. At one-and-a-half the two crossings meet at a tidy 45°,
and each swing works out to three-quarters of a turn — which is where that number
in the next step comes from.

So a 14-inch circle wants the Earth and Moon about 21 inches apart. If you picked
a big circle in the last activity, spread them out to match.

</Callout>

### Step 2 · Build the mission

Four moves, and you already have the numbers for two of them:

| # | Move | Block | The number |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Orbit the Moon | Arcade + Sleep | your Straight and Turn |
| 2 | Cross to the Earth | Straight | the gap, in cm |
| 3 | Orbit the Earth, the other way | Arcade + Sleep | same, with Turn **negated** |
| 4 | Cross back to the Moon | Straight | the same gap |

For the orbits, use a Sleep of about **three-quarters** of a full circle — 1.5
times your half-circle time. That is not a guess: with the bodies spaced as above,
three-quarters of a turn is exactly what brings the robot out of a swing pointing
at the other body. Tune from there rather than starting from nothing.

Notice which blocks do which job — it's why the picture above draws them
differently. The crossings use `Straight`, which **finishes**: it drives a measured
distance and stops itself, so it lands the same way every run, and it's drawn
solid. The swings have to use Arcade, which doesn't finish, so they need a Sleep to
say when to stop, and they're drawn dashed because a time is a much shakier thing
to rely on than a distance. That's the distinction from earlier in this lesson,
doing real work in one program.

### Step 3 · Fly it, then fix it

It will not work first time. That's the job:

- **Overshooting the turn round the Moon** — the robot leaves pointing the wrong
  way. Shorten that Sleep.
- **Missing the Earth entirely** — the crossing distance is wrong, or the orbit
  before it left the robot off-heading. Fix the heading first; the distance can't
  be right until the direction is.
- **A lopsided path** — the two Turn values aren't true mirror images. Check the
  sign is the *only* thing that changed between them.

**You've succeeded when** the marker traces a path that loops the Moon, crosses to
the Earth, loops it the other way, and comes back to meet where it started.

**Challenge** — move the two bodies further apart without changing the size of
your orbits. Which of the four numbers has to change, and which don't?

<Callout kind="tip" title="No whiteboard panel or cutouts?">

Two sticky notes on the floor work, or two chalk marks. You lose the drawn path,
so judge it by eye instead: does the robot pass close to each marker, and does it
finish where it began? Everything else about the activity is the same.

</Callout>

<TeacherNote title="Running Earth – Moon – Earth">

**Why this framing.** It's the same figure-eight the shape has always been, but a
mission gives students a reason to care whether the robot arrives *pointing the
right way*, which is the part that actually teaches heading. "Your eight is
lopsided" gets a shrug; "you missed the Earth" does not.

**Space:** with 12–18 inch orbits and the bodies 18–24 inches apart, the whole
path fits in roughly 1 m × 0.5 m — a desk or one panel. Groups who fell in love
with a huge circle in the last activity will need to go back and find a smaller
one, which is a fair consequence rather than a failure.

**Step 1 is not optional.** Students who skip the drawing tune four numbers at
once with no idea which one is wrong. The drawing tells them what *should* happen,
so a wrong run becomes information.

The first flight rarely closes, and that is the useful part. Two robots with
identical numbers fly visibly different missions, and the same robot differs on a
fresh battery. Name it: *the only reason we're timing this is that the robot can't
yet sense where it is.* Module 2 picks that thread up, and by now students have
felt it three times.

**If you want the science:** the Apollo missions really did fly a figure-eight.
The free-return trajectory loops behind the Moon and lets the Moon's gravity throw
the spacecraft back toward Earth, so that if the engines fail, the crew comes home
anyway. It's what saved Apollo 13. Worth ninety seconds, not a lesson.

</TeacherNote>

## Wrap-up

- How does a differential-drive robot turn? (By running its wheels at different speeds.)
- What happens to the motors when a Straight block finishes? What about a Set effort block? (Straight stops them; Set effort leaves them running.)
- Why is timed driving unreliable, and what should a robot stop on instead? (Effort isn't speed — battery and surface change the distance; stop on a sensor reading.)
- When would you pick the `Straight`/`Turn` blocks over `Set effort`? (When you need precise, repeatable distances and angles.)
- In the Arcade block, which number changes the size of the circle, and which way? (Turn — bigger Turn, tighter circle.)
