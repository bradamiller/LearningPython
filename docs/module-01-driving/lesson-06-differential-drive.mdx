Every `Straight` and `Turn` you've used is really controlling two motors behind
the scenes. In this lesson you'll take the wheel yourself — driving the left and
right motors **directly** to make curves, spins, and smooth steering that the
high-level blocks can't do on their own.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain **differential drive** — steering by running the two wheels at different speeds
- Use **Set effort** to control the left and right motors directly (`-1` to `1`)
- Use the **Arcade** block for smooth, game-controller-style steering
- Decide when to use direct motor control vs. the high-level `Straight`/`Turn`

</Objectives>

## Part 1 · How the robot really steers

The XRP is a **differential drive** robot: it has no steering wheel. It turns by
running its two wheels at different speeds. Same speed both sides → straight.
One side faster → it curves. One forward and one backward → it spins on the spot.

<Figure
  placeholderLabel="Diagram — three cases: equal efforts (straight), unequal (curve), opposite (spin)"
  caption="Different left/right efforts produce every kind of motion."
/>

The **Set effort** block gives you that direct control. Effort runs from `-1`
(full reverse) through `0` (stopped) to `1` (full forward), and you set each wheel
separately. Unlike `Straight`, the motors keep running until you tell them to
stop — so **Stop motors** is required.

  caption="Drive straight for 3 seconds using direct motor control."

## Part 2 · The most common bug: forgetting to stop

With `Straight`, the robot stops itself. With `Set effort`, it doesn't — the
motors hold their last effort until you change them. Leave off **Stop motors** and
your robot drives off the table.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="Your Set effort program makes the robot drive away and never stop. What did you most likely forget?"
  options={[
    {text: 'The Wait for button press block'},
    {text: 'The Stop motors block', correct: true},
    {text: 'To charge the battery'},
    {text: 'The Turn block'},
  ]}
  explanation="Set effort runs the motors continuously. Without a Stop motors block, they keep going at the last effort you set."
/>

<TeacherNote title="Straight vs. Set effort — the trade-off">

This is the lesson's big idea: **abstraction vs. control**. `Straight`/`Turn` use
the robot's sensors (encoders/IMU) for precise, repeatable distances and angles —
easy, but limited to straight lines and pivots. `Set effort` is raw and
time-based: less precise, but it can make smooth curves and creative motion the
high-level blocks can't. Neither is "better" — it's about the right tool for the
task. Reinforce **Stop motors** relentlessly; forgetting it is the #1 issue today.

</TeacherNote>

## Part 3 · Smooth steering with Arcade

Setting two efforts by hand is fiddly. The **Arcade** block is friendlier — you
give it a forward **speed** and a **turn** amount (like a game controller), and it
works out the two motor efforts for you.

<BlockProgram
  blocks={[
    {name: 'wait_for_button_press'},
    {name: 'arcade', note: 'speed: 0.5, turn: 0.3  → forward, curving right'},
    {name: 'sleep', note: '3 seconds'},
    {name: 'stop_motors'},
  ]}
  caption="Arcade combines speed and steering into one intuitive block."
/>

Try it: drive a big curve, then a figure-eight (curve one way, then the other).

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="When is the Arcade block a better choice than Set effort?"
  options={[
    {text: 'When you need smooth, intuitive steering like a game controller', correct: true},
    {text: 'When you need to control each wheel to an exact effort'},
    {text: 'When you want the robot to stop by itself'},
    {text: 'Arcade and Set effort are exactly the same'},
  ]}
  explanation="Arcade is easiest for smooth curves and navigation; Set effort gives finer, per-wheel control when you need it."
/>

<TeacherNote title="Differentiation">

**More support:** give a table of effort pairs and their outcomes, and start with
symmetric efforts (0.3/0.3) before curves; let students use Arcade first — it's
more intuitive. **More challenge:** build a spiral by gradually increasing one
side's effort across several Set effort blocks, or estimate a fixed distance using
effort + time (and see why encoders are worth having).

</TeacherNote>

## Wrap-up

- How does a differential-drive robot turn? (By running its wheels at different speeds.)
- Why must a Set effort program include Stop motors? (The motors run until told to stop.)
- When would you pick `Straight`/`Turn` over `Set effort`? (When you need precise, repeatable distances and angles.)
