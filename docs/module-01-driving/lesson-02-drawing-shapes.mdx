In Lesson 1 you drove the robot forward and back. Now you'll make it draw whole
shapes — squares, triangles, pentagons — using one powerful idea: **repeating**
a drive-and-turn a few times.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Use the **Repeat** block to run commands over and over without retyping them
- Combine **Straight** and **Turn** to trace the sides of a shape
- Calculate the turn angle for any regular polygon (`360° ÷ number of sides`)
- Predict and debug a shape by changing the repeat count and the angle

</Objectives>

## Part 1 · Why a square is really four repeats

Watch how the robot draws a square, and notice the pattern: drive a side, turn a
corner — four times.

Instead of dragging four Straight blocks and four Turn blocks, you can wrap **one**

## Part 2 · The angle trick

Here's the one piece of math you need. To close any regular shape, the robot makes
one full turn (360°) split evenly across the corners:

> **Turn angle = 360° ÷ number of sides**

A square has 4 sides, so each turn is `360 ÷ 4 = 90°`. A triangle has 3 sides, so
`360 ÷ 3 = 120°`.

  ]}
  explanation="360 ÷ 6 = 60°. Split one full turn evenly across all six corners."
/>

  question="A classmate's triangle isn't closing up — the robot ends facing the wrong way. They used Turn 60°. What should it be?"

    {text: '90°'},

<TeacherNote title="Head off the interior-angle mistake">

The most common error all lesson is students using the *interior* angle (a
triangle's 60°) instead of the *exterior* turn (120°). Emphasize that the robot
turns through the outside angle, and that all the turns together add up to one
full 360° rotation. A quick "walk the square" demo — students physically pacing
and pivoting 90° at each corner — makes it stick.

</TeacherNote>

## Part 3 · On paper first

Before you touch a single block, work it out by hand — it's much faster to fix a
wrong angle on paper than on a robot.

1. **Draw the square.** On your worksheet, sketch a square and walk the robot
   around it with your pencil: at each corner, write the turn angle. All four
   turns should add up to `360°`.
2. **Fill in the table.** For each shape, compute the turn angle with
   `360 ÷ sides`:

   | Shape | Sides | Repeat | Turn angle |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Triangle | 3 | 3 | ____ |
   | Square | 4 | 4 | ____ |
   | Pentagon | 5 | 5 | ____ |
   | Hexagon | 6 | 6 | ____ |

3. **Predict.** Write down what the robot will draw for *Repeat 4 · Turn 90*
   before you run it. Then check yourself against the real thing in Part 4.

## Part 4 · Build it and drive it

1. Start a new project and build the square program above (Repeat 4 → Straight
   30, Turn 90).
2. Give the robot clear floor space, upload, and press its button to run.
3. Watch it trace a square and end up roughly where it started, facing its start
   direction.

**You've succeeded when** the robot drives four roughly-equal sides and turns four
corners, ending near its starting spot.

<TeacherNote title="Space, effort, and clean shapes">

Give each robot a clear patch of floor (a 1–1.5 m square is plenty at 30 cm
sides). If shapes come out lopsided, it's usually wheel slip — lower **Effort**
to `0.3` on carpet, or nudge it up to `0.7` if the robot is sluggish. Shapes
within ~10% are a success; perfect closure isn't the goal here.

</TeacherNote>

## Part 5 · Make other shapes

Now change just two numbers — the repeat count and the turn angle — to draw new
shapes. Use the angles from your table in Part 3, and try them in order:

1. **Triangle** — Repeat `3`, Turn `120°`, Straight `25`.
2. **Pentagon** — Repeat `5`, Turn `72°`, Straight `20`.
3. **Your choice** — pick a number of sides, compute `360 ÷ sides`, and predict
   the shape before you run it.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="You change a working square program from Repeat 4 to Repeat 6, and set the turn to 60°. What does the robot draw?"

/>

<TeacherNote title="Differentiation">

**More support:** hand out a reference card of pre-computed angles (3→120°,
4→90°, 5→72°, 6→60°, 8→45°) and a fill-in template: "Repeat (__) · Straight (__)
· Turn (__)." **More challenge:** have them draw a star (5 points, 144° turns) or
combine two shapes back-to-back from the same start point.

</TeacherNote>

## Real-world connections

Repeating a move-and-turn is exactly how machines make precise, repeatable paths:

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Manufacturing" title="CNC machines">
    Cutting tools trace shapes by repeating precise move-and-turn steps, just at much finer scale.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Making" title="3D printers">
    Each layer is a looped path the print head repeats hundreds of times to build up an object.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Art" title="Pen plotters">
    Drawing robots turn loops of coordinates into logos, maps, and signatures on paper.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

## Wrap-up

- Why does a Repeat block make shape code shorter? (One copy of Straight + Turn instead of many.)
- What's the rule for the turn angle? (`360 ÷ number of sides`.)
- If a shape doesn't close, what's the first thing to check? (The turn angle.)
