You're in a robotics class, so you should probably be able to say what a robot
*is*. That turns out to be harder than it sounds. Before you touch the XRP, you'll
look at a lineup of machines — some obviously robots, some obviously not, and a few
that will split the room — and argue it out until a definition emerges.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Argue whether a given device is or isn't a robot, and say *why*
- Work out, from the examples, what every robot has to be able to do — and state it as a short definition
- Explain why the classic "mechanical arm" definition isn't enough anymore
- Describe how the XRP — and this course — fits that definition

</Objectives>

## Part 1 · It used to be easy to tell

For most of the last century, a robot was easy to spot: a metal figure with a
visor, walking stiffly out of a flying saucer in a black-and-white movie. Nobody
argued about whether *that* was a robot.

<Video
  src="/videos/gort.mp4"
  mp4
  caption="Gort, from The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951) — the robot everyone recognizes. Today the line is a lot blurrier."
/>

Today the machines around us are smarter and much less obvious. So: is the thing
in your pocket a robot? The car in the driveway? The washing machine? Let's find
out where *you* draw the line.

<TeacherNote title="How to run this lesson (30–40 min)">

This is a **discussion**, not a lecture. Don't reveal answers as you go — the
value is in the disagreement. A format that works: show one device at a time, have
students **vote with their feet** ("robot" on one side of the room, "not a robot"
on the other, "not sure" in the middle), then call on someone from each side to
defend their position. Keep a running list on the board of the *reasons* people
give ("it moves on its own," "a person is controlling it," "it has sensors") —
you'll harvest those reasons into the definition in Part 3. The two things to
listen for and amplify are **sensors** and **acting on what was sensed**: every
device students call a robot senses something and decides what to do about it;
every "not a robot" has a person doing the sensing and deciding. Don't name
those traits for them — the student page deliberately doesn't. Use the open
"Think about it" prompt under each picture (most ask *what happens if the person
walks away*), and keep asking "what's your reason?" until sensing and deciding
come out of their mouths. Budget roughly 15
minutes for the gallery, 10 for the definition, 5–10 for the wrap-up.

</TeacherNote>

## Part 2 · Is this a robot?

You're about to see eight devices. For **each one**, before your class discusses
it:

1. **Decide:** robot or not a robot. (You have to pick — no "not sure.")
2. **Say why.** Write one sentence: *what is it about this device* that makes it
   a robot, or keeps it from being one?

Keep a running table as you go — you'll need it in Part 3:

| Device | Robot? (yes / no) | Why? — what about this device makes it a robot, or keeps it from being one? |
|---|---|---|
| RC airplane | | |
| Camera drone | | |
| Vacuum cleaner | | |

| Car | | |

### A radio-controlled airplane

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/RadioControlledAirplane.jpg"
  alt="A radio-controlled model airplane next to its handheld transmitter"
  caption="It flies. It has motors. Somebody's holding a controller. Robot?"
/>

**Think about it:** If the pilot sets the controller down, what does the plane do next?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · RC plane">

Most students say **not a robot**, and the reason they give is the key one: *a
person is doing all the deciding.* The plane senses nothing and decides nothing —
the human does the sensing (eyes) and the thinking (hands on the sticks). Push
back gently: "But it has motors and it moves — isn't that enough?" You want them
to articulate that *moving* isn't the test.

</TeacherNote>

### A camera drone

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/Drone.jpg"
  alt="A folding camera drone with four rotors"
  caption="Also flies, also has a controller in the box. Same answer as the airplane?"
/>

**Think about it:** Also flies, also comes with a controller. If the pilot lets go of the sticks, what happens? If your answer is different from the airplane's, what makes the difference?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Drone">

This is the pair to the airplane, and it's where the room starts to split. A
modern camera drone has GPS, obstacle sensors, and a computer that holds it
level, stops it from hitting a wall, and flies itself home when the battery is
low — *even though a human is nominally controlling it.* Ask: "What can the drone
do that the airplane can't?" The answer — it senses and makes some decisions on
its own — is the first crack in the "someone's controlling it, so it's not a
robot" argument.

</TeacherNote>

### A vacuum cleaner

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/ManualVacuum.jpg"
  alt="A canister vacuum cleaner with a hose and floor head"
  caption="Powerful motor, does a useful job. Robot?"
/>

**Think about it:** Who steers it? If you walk away in the middle of cleaning, what happens?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Canister vacuum">

Easy **no**, and useful for the same reason as the airplane: a person pushes it
everywhere. It has a motor and a job, but no sensing, no deciding. Keep it quick;
it's here to set up the next one.

</TeacherNote>

### A vacuum that drives itself

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/RobotVacuum.jpg"
  alt="A round vacuum driving a back-and-forth pattern across a wood floor"
  caption="Same job as the vacuum above. Different answer?"
/>

**Think about it:** Same job as the vacuum above. What's different about *how* it does the job? What happens if you walk away?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Robot vacuum">

Nearly everyone says **yes** — it's even *called* a robot vacuum. Ask them to
prove it: what does it sense (bumper, cliff sensors so it doesn't fall down
stairs, dirt detection), what does it decide (turn when it hits something, go
back to the dock when the battery's low), what does it do (drive, vacuum). Same
job as the canister vacuum, but it senses, decides, and acts *on its own*. Put
those three words on the board — they're about to become the definition.

</TeacherNote>

### A car

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/RedCar.jpg"
  alt="A red sedan parked with a city skyline behind it"
  caption="A car. Is a car a robot?"
/>

**Think about it:** Answer once with a person driving. Now answer again with the car in self-driving mode. Did your answer change? If so, what changed about the *car*?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Self-driving-capable car">

Expect a genuine split, and let it run. If a person is driving, most say no. If
the car is on driver-assist or self-driving mode — cameras sense the road, a
computer decides to brake or steer, and it steers — most say yes. The interesting
conclusion: *the same machine can be a robot or not depending on what's doing the
thinking.* That's a sophisticated point; if a student gets there on their own,
celebrate it.

</TeacherNote>

### A laundry-folding machine

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/ClothesFolding.jpg"
  alt="A two-armed machine folding a towel on a table"
  caption="A research robot folding laundry."
/>

**Think about it:** Most people say yes right away — so say *precisely* what makes it one. What would it have to know about the towel in order to fold it?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Towel-folding robot">

An easy **yes** and a chance to name *why* it's hard: folding a floppy towel means
sensing where the cloth is (cameras), figuring out how to grab it (that's the
hard part), and moving arms precisely. Note that a task trivial for a human is a
research problem for a robot — a theme students will feel personally in Module 2
when line-following turns out to be harder than it looks.

</TeacherNote>

### A Mars rover

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/MarsRover.jpg"
  alt="A rover on the surface of Mars"
  caption="A rover on Mars, hundreds of millions of kilometers away."
/>

**Think about it:** A radio signal to Mars takes several minutes each way. What does that mean for how the rover gets driven? Compare with the RC airplane.

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Mars rover">

**Yes** — and the twist is worth stating: a radio signal to Mars takes several
minutes each way, so nobody can drive it with a joystick. It *has* to look at the
ground, decide whether a rock is safe, and drive around it by itself. Ask: "Why
couldn't NASA just remote-control it like the airplane?" Distance forces autonomy
— a great example of why "thinking for itself" is the heart of the definition.

</TeacherNote>

### A washing machine

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-01/WashingMachine.jpg"
  alt="A front-loading washing machine with towels in the open door"
  caption="A washing machine. Think carefully before you answer."
/>

**Think about it:** Press start and walk away. What does it do on its own? Now look at what you wrote for the vacuum that drives itself — are you being consistent?

<TeacherNote title="Talking points · Washing machine (the one that breaks the room)">

Save this for last — it's the productive controversy. Students say "obviously not"
… until someone points out it senses the load size and water level, follows cycle
logic (it *decides* when to spin, how long to rinse), and acts (fills, agitates,
spins) — all without a human after the button press. By the criteria on the board,
it might qualify! Don't resolve it. The honest takeaway is that "robot" is a
**spectrum**, not a switch: a washing machine does all three things in a very
simple, fixed way; a rover does them in a rich, adaptable way. Both are further
along the line than an RC plane. The point of a definition is to make the
argument *precise*, not to end it.

</TeacherNote>

Before reading on, look down the **Why?** column of your table. What do your
"yes" reasons have in common? What do your "no" reasons have in common? Try to
write the rule in one sentence — *"A device is a robot if it ______."* Then see
how close you got.

## Part 3 · So what *is* a robot?

Here's the definition engineers wrote back in **1979** (the Robot Institute of
America): a robot is *"a reprogrammable, multifunctional manipulator designed to
move material, parts, tools, or specialized devices through various programmed
motions for the performance of a variety of tasks."*

In plain English: a **programmable mechanical arm** that does factory work —
assembly, welding, painting. The classic jobs are the **three Ds**: work that's
**dirty, dull, or dangerous** for people.

That definition was fine for 1979. But check it against Part 2: a Mars rover isn't
a "manipulator," and neither is a drone or a robot vacuum. The definition is too
narrow for the robots we actually have. Let's try again.

### Try again: a better definition

The robotics engineering program at WPI uses three traits instead. A robot must:

1. **Sense** — take in information about the world with some kind of sensor.
2. **Think** — process what it sensed; make a decision based on it.
3. **Act** — do something in the world based on that decision: move, grab,
   change something.

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="1 · Sense" title="Take in the world">
    Cameras, bump sensors, distance sensors, GPS, light sensors — some way of finding out what's out there.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="2 · Think" title="Decide">
    A program that turns sensor readings into a choice: turn left, stop, go home, grab here.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="3 · Act" title="Change the world">
    Motors, wheels, arms, propellers — something that carries the decision out physically.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

**A robot must sense, think, and act.** Miss one and it's something else: a
machine that only acts is a tool (the vacuum); a machine where a *person* does the
sensing and thinking is remote-controlled (the airplane).

<TeacherNote title="Harvesting the board into the definition">

Before showing the three traits, have students compare "Why?" columns. Ask what
the "yes" reasons share and what the "no" reasons share. Group their words on the
board into *sense / think / act* — you're not handing them a definition, you're
naming the one they built. The punchline to draw out: *every* device on the list
moves or acts, so acting can't be the test; what separates the robots is that
they sense something and act on what they sensed. If a student says "it's not
about moving, it's about sensing and deciding," you're done — write it on the
board in their words. The spectrum idea (washing machine at one end, rover at the other)
is the most important nuance; it inoculates against the "is it or isn't it"
dead-end and sets up the idea that this course moves the XRP *along* that
spectrum.

</TeacherNote>

## Part 4 · Why robots — and where the XRP fits

Robots are worth building because they combine so many ideas at once. A
competition robot built by high-school students **senses** where the balls and
the goal are with a camera, **thinks** — using trigonometry to aim — and
**acts**, driving into position and shooting. A climbing robot needs torque, gear
ratios, and speed calculations just to get off the ground. Robotics is where math,
physics, and programming stop being separate subjects.

<Video
  src="/videos/ballshooter.mp4"
  mp4
  caption="A FIRST competition robot built by high-school students: it senses where the balls and goal are with a camera, thinks (trigonometry to aim), and acts — shooting into the goal."
/>

Now look at the robot on your desk with those three words in mind:

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Sense" title="The XRP's senses">
    Reflectance sensors that see light vs. dark, an ultrasonic rangefinder that measures distance, and encoders that count how far each wheel has turned.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Think" title="The XRP's brain">
    The controller board runs <em>your</em> program. Every decision it makes is one you wrote — in Blockly first, then in Python.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Act" title="The XRP's muscles">
    Two motors, one per wheel. That's enough to drive straight, turn, curve, and spin.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

And this is exactly the shape of the course. In **Module 1** you'll mostly teach
the XRP to **act** — driving, turning, drawing shapes. In **Module 2** you'll add
**sensing**, so it can follow a line and react to what it sees. By **Modules 4
and 5** the **thinking** gets serious: your robot will plan its own route across a
grid and re-plan around obstacles it discovers. By the end, the XRP will be doing
all three — a robot by anyone's definition.

The XRP isn't a toy, either — the same robot is used in a college sophomore
programming course. The tools you'll learn here are the real ones.

## Real-world connections

Robots earn their keep on the **three Ds** — work that's dirty, dull, or dangerous:

## Wrap-up

- What three things must a robot do? (Sense, think, act.)
- Why isn't a remote-controlled plane a robot? (The person does the sensing and thinking.)
- Name a device at home and argue whether it's a robot — using all three traits.

<TeacherNote title="Assessment & next lesson">

**Formative:** can students give a *reason*, not just a vote, for each device, and
map the reason onto sense/think/act? **Exit ticket:** "Pick any device in your

spectrum idea — "it senses and acts but doesn't really decide anything.")
