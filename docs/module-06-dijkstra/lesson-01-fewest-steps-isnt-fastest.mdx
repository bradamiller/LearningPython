Module 5's planner finds the path with the fewest steps, and on your grid so far that
has always been the fastest path too — every block took the same time. Today one block
doesn't. Your teacher has changed one segment of the grid so the tape takes a long
detour between two neighboring intersections. The map still says one step. Your robot
will take a lot longer than one step to drive it. This lesson is about measuring that,
and turning it into a number the planner can use.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why the shortest path in steps is not always the fastest in time
- Time a normal block and the slow segment with a stopwatch, and turn the ratio into a **weight**
- Add up the cost of a route by hand, with and without the slow segment
- Predict which of two routes BFS will choose, and whether it is the faster one
- Say why a slow segment that costs exactly three blocks is not slow enough to matter

</Objectives>

## The grid has a slow road

Look at the grid. One segment between two adjacent intersections is no longer a
straight piece of tape — it winds back and forth before it arrives. Line following
still works on it; the sensors don't care that the tape wiggles. But the robot has to
drive the whole length of the wiggle.

To the Module 5 graph, that segment is still one edge: `(0, 1)` is a neighbor of
`(0, 2)`, full stop. `build_graph()` has no way to say "this neighbor is far." And
`compute_bfs_path()` counts edges. So from (0, 0) to (0, 3) it will pick the straight
row — three steps — and send the robot down the slow road, every time.

<KnowledgeCheck

## Activity · Measure it

A planner needs a number, not "it's slow." Get one with a stopwatch:

1. Time the robot driving one **normal** block, intersection to intersection, with
   `track_to_intersection()`. Do it three times and average.
2. Time the robot driving the **slow** segment the same way, three times, average.
3. Divide: slow time ÷ normal time. That ratio is the segment's **weight** — how many
   normal blocks' worth of time it costs.

```
Normal block:   4.1 s   4.0 s   4.2 s    average 4.1 s
Slow segment:  19.8 s  20.5 s  20.1 s    average 20.1 s

weight = 20.1 / 4.1 = 4.9  -> call it 5
```

Round to a whole number. The planner doesn't need two decimal places; it needs to know
that this edge is about five times worse than the others. Write your weight down —
Lesson 6-2 types it into the program.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="A normal block takes 4 seconds and the slow segment takes 16. What is its weight?"
  options={[
    {text: '16'},
    {text: '4 — it costs four normal blocks\' worth of time', correct: true},
    {text: '12'},
    {text: '0.25'},
  ]}
  explanation="weight = slow time / normal time = 16 / 4 = 4. A weight is measured in blocks, not seconds, so the same number works whatever speed the robot drives at."
/>

## Cost on paper

Now compare two routes from (0, 0) to (0, 3) on a 4×4 grid, with the slow segment
between (0, 1) and (0, 2) at weight 5. A route's **cost** is the sum of its edge
weights — 1 for a normal block, 5 for the slow one.

```
Route A, straight along row 0:
  (0,0) -> (0,1) -> (0,2) -> (0,3)
  cost =    1   +    5   +    1    = 7       (3 steps)

Route B, down and around through row 1:
  (0,0) -> (1,0) -> (1,1) -> (1,2) -> (1,3) -> (0,3)
  cost =    1   +    1   +    1   +    1   +    1    = 5       (5 steps)
```

Route A has fewer steps. Route B is faster. BFS picks A.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="With the slow segment at weight 5, which route does BFS choose from (0,0) to (0,3), and what does it cost?"
  options={[
    {text: 'Route B, cost 5 — BFS finds the cheapest path'},
    {text: 'Route A, cost 7 — BFS finds the fewest steps, and A has 3 steps to B\'s 5', correct: true},
    {text: 'Route A, cost 3'},
    {text: 'Neither; it reports no path'},
  ]}
  explanation="BFS compares 3 steps against 5 steps and takes 3. It never sees the 7, because cost is not something it computes. The robot pays the extra time on the floor."
/>

## Why three isn't enough

Try the same comparison with the slow segment at weight **3**. Route A costs
1 + 3 + 1 = 5. Route B still costs 5. They tie — and going round one segment on an
open grid always takes exactly three normal blocks, so a weight of three is the
break-even point. For the two planners in this module to *disagree*, the slow segment
has to be clearly worse than the detour around it: a weight of 4 or more. If your
stopwatch gave you 3, ask your teacher to make the detour longer.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="The slow segment measures weight 3. From (0,0) to (0,3), what happens?"
  options={[
    {text: 'The detour is faster by 2'},
    {text: 'The straight route costs 1+3+1 = 5 and the detour costs 5 — a tie, so the slow road is not slow enough to change the answer', correct: true},
    {text: 'The straight route is faster by 2'},
    {text: 'BFS reports no path'},
  ]}
  explanation="Going round one segment costs three normal blocks on an open grid, so weight 3 exactly breaks even. The module needs the slow segment to be worse than that."
/>

<TeacherNote title="Set the grid up first, and make the detour long">

Do this before class: re-tape one segment between two adjacent intersections as a
winding detour long enough that the drive takes at least four normal blocks' time
(five is better; it makes the numbers unambiguous). Keep the detour's tape the same
width and the curves gentle enough for line following; test it with
`track_to_intersection()` before students arrive. The two intersections it joins
must still read as crosses, and the segment should be on an obvious shortest-steps
route — between (0,1) and (0,2) on a 4×4 grid, with trips along row 0, works well.

The stopwatch activity is the heart of the lesson. The weight has to come from a
measurement students made, or the module is just arithmetic. Three timings each and
an average also quietly revisits why we measure more than once.

If your detour comes out at exactly weight 3, the module falls flat (the tie above).
Lengthen it.

**Physical setup is still being tried on a real grid.** The winding detour is the
leading option because it needs nothing under the tape and does not disturb the
reflectance sensors; if you find a better one, note it in the source repository's
`module-06-dijkstra/README.md`.

</TeacherNote>

## Real-world connections

Every real router works in time or cost, not hops:

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Maps" title="Fastest, not shortest">
    A map app's route is rarely the fewest turns or the fewest miles; it is the least time, with each road weighted by its speed.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Networks" title="Link costs">
    Internet routers weight links by bandwidth and delay; a path with more hops over fast links beats a short path over a slow one.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Logistics" title="Toll roads and traffic">
    Delivery routing adds tolls, traffic and loading time to distance. The cheapest route is the one with the smallest total.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

## Wrap-up

- What does a weight mean? (How many normal blocks' worth of time a segment costs.)
- How do you get one? (Time the slow segment, divide by a normal block, round.)
- What is a route's cost? (The sum of its edge weights.)
- Why isn't weight 3 enough? (The detour around one segment is 3 blocks; it ties.)
