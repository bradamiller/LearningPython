Module 2 ended with a robot that can follow a line, find a cross, park on it and turn
onto the next line. That was all on one taped circle. In this module the floor becomes
a **grid**: taped lines running across and up, crossing each other all over.

By the end of the module your robot will **drive a square around the grid**, two
intersections per side with a right turn at every corner, and finish exactly where it
started. It's a short module, four lessons, because you already wrote the hard part.

## Reuse is the whole idea

You'd expect a new kind of floor to need a new program. It doesn't. The two sections
of `robot.py` you finished in Module 2, LINE SENSOR and LINE TRACKING, already know how
to follow a line, stop at a cross and turn onto the next line, and a grid is made of
nothing else. They come across into this module **unchanged**.

What's new is a third section, **GRID DRIVING**, and it's small: a way to say "drive
through three intersections" in one line, and tests that string drives and turns into
routes. None of it reads a sensor. It only calls the functions you already trust.

That's how real programs get built: write a piece well once, test it, then use it for
things you hadn't thought of when you wrote it.

## Routes in intersections, not centimeters

On a grid, a route is a list of instructions like *drive two intersections, turn right,
drive one intersection.* Every number in it is a count of intersections, so it means
the same thing on any grid, whatever the spacing between the lines. And because every
stop happens on an intersection the sensors found, the robot knows exactly where it is
after each one.

<TeacherNote title="This is an advance organizer — keep it short">

Module 3 is the lightest module, and the one to compress if you're short on time, so
this page is short too. The two ideas to leave on the board are **reuse** (no new
driving code: say it before Lesson 3-1 does, so students notice when it comes true)
and **who plans the route** (you now, the robot from Module 4 on). Don't explain why a
grid intersection looks like the cross; Lesson 3-1 opens with exactly that.

**Tape the grid before Lesson 3-1.** It's the 4 × 4 grid in
[Setting up](/teacher-guide/setting-up), and it stays down for Modules 3 to 6, so it's
worth taping carefully once. Lessons 3-1 to 3-4 use only part of it.

</TeacherNote>

## Your roadmap

| Lesson | What happens |
|---|---|
| **3-1 · Intro to the Grid** | Put the robot on the grid and drive to the first intersection with the functions you already have. The GRID DRIVING section opens. |
| **3-2 · Multiple Intersections** | Drive through a chosen number of intersections with one call: the section's first function. |
| **3-3 · Turning on the Grid** | Turn corners: drive, turn, drive, tracing an L-shaped path you planned on paper. |
| **3-4 · Final Project** | Drive a square, two intersections per side, and end where you started. |

## Wrap-up

- What will the robot do by the end of the module? (Drive a square on the grid and finish where it started.)
- Why is there so little new driving code? (LINE SENSOR and LINE TRACKING already handle lines, crosses and turns, and a grid is made of those.)
- Who plans the route in Module 3? (You do. In Module 4, the robot will.)
