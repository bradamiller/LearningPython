## Drive, turn, drive

Turning a corner on the grid is just like walking city streets: go to the
intersection, then turn. In code that's `drive_intersections()`, then a turn, then
`drive_intersections()` again — three lines for an L, one per leg or corner.

## Why the turn lands on the new line

Look at what `drive_intersections()` leaves behind: the robot parked with its wheels
over the last intersection, sensors just past it. That is exactly the position a turn
needs. The robot pivots about its center of rotation (Lesson 3-1), so with that
point on the cross it spins *on* the intersection, and the blind 60° plus the sensor-ended finish from Lesson 2-9
bring the sensors down on the crossing line. After the turn, the next
`drive_intersections()` starts with a line to follow — no extra step in between.

<TeacherNote title="Two rules that trip students up">

Reinforce two things: (1) the robot must be **parked on an intersection** to turn —
you can't turn mid-segment, and `drive_intersections()` always leaves it parked; and
(2) the arrival pair is `track_to_intersection()` *then* `drive_past_intersection()`,
every time — a turn before the drive-past pivots in the wrong place. Students who
hand-write the pair sometimes drop the second half; `drive_intersections()` exists
partly so they can't.

</TeacherNote>

### Step 1 · test_l_shape()

Write the body from its comment — three calls and a print — set the run line to
`test_l_shape()`, and run it. Start the robot on a line segment with at least two
intersections ahead and room to the right. It should drive two segments, pivot on the
second intersection, drive two more along the crossing line, and stop parked on the
fourth intersection it met. Don't go on until it does.

### Step 2 · Plan on paper, then test_my_path()

Before coding a path, sketch it: mark the start, the direction the robot faces, how
many segments each leg is, and where the turns go. A plan reads like directions —
*leg 1: three segments; corner: left; leg 2: one segment* — and translates one leg
or corner per line, with a comment saying which is which. Write that into
`test_my_path()`, set the run line to it, and run. Did the robot end where your
sketch says? If not, the sketch and the code disagree somewhere; find the line.

<Reveal title="Check your L-shape" hint="run test_l_shape() first">

```python
def test_l_shape():
    drive_intersections(2)      # leg 1
    turn_clockwise()            # corner: right
    drive_intersections(2)      # leg 2
    print("L-shape complete!")
```

`test_my_path()` is yours, so there is nothing to check it against but your sketch.
For the plan above it would be `drive_intersections(3)`, `turn_counterclockwise()`,
`drive_intersections(1)`.

</Reveal>

```python
def test_l_shape():

    drive_intersections(2)      # leg 2
    print("L-shape complete!")

def test_my_path():
    drive_intersections(3)      # leg 1
    turn_counterclockwise()     # corner: left
    drive_intersections(1)      # leg 2
    print("Path complete!")

# ===== RUN =====
board.wait_for_button()
test_my_path()
```

**More support:** give students a drawn path and have them write the matching

## Wrap-up

- What's the basic shape of any grid path? (A sequence of drives and turns.)
- What has to come before a turn? (The arrival pair — the robot parked on the intersection.)
- What comes after a turn? (The next drive — a sensor is already on the new line.)
