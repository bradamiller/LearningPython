You can drive straight down the grid. Now you'll turn corners — sequencing drives and
turns to trace an **L-shaped path**. Any route across a grid is really just a
sequence of "drive some segments, turn, drive some more."

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Sequence drive and turn operations into a path
- Turn at intersections with `turn_clockwise()` and `turn_counterclockwise()`, and say why the turn pivots on the intersection
- Navigate an L-shaped path
- Plan a path on paper before you write the code

</Objectives>

## Drive, turn, drive

Turning a corner on the grid is just like walking city streets: go to the
intersection, then turn. In code that's `drive_intersections()`, then a turn, then
`drive_intersections()` again:

```python
# (both Module 2 toolkits and drive_intersections from Lesson 3-2 go above)

board = Board.get_default_board()

board.wait_for_button()

drive_intersections(2)      # drive forward 2 intersections
turn_clockwise()            # turn right onto the crossing line
drive_intersections(2)      # drive 2 more, now sideways

print("L-shape complete!")
```

## Why the turn lands on the new line

Look at what `drive_intersections()` leaves behind: the robot parked with its wheels
over the last intersection, sensors just past it. That is exactly the position a turn
needs. The robot pivots about its wheels, so with the wheels on the cross it spins
*on* the intersection, and the blind 60° plus the sensor-ended finish from Lesson 2-9
bring the sensors down on the crossing line. After the turn, the next
`drive_intersections()` starts with a line to follow — no extra step in between.

/>

<TeacherNote title="Two rules that trip students up">

Reinforce two things: (1) the robot must be **parked on an intersection** to turn —
you can't turn mid-segment, and `drive_intersections()` always leaves it parked; and
(2) the arrival pair is `track_to_intersection()` *then* `drive_past_intersection()`,
every time — a turn before the drive-past pivots in the wrong place. Students who
hand-write the pair sometimes drop the second half; `drive_intersections()` exists
partly so they can't.

</TeacherNote>

## Activity · Plan on paper first

Before coding a path, sketch it: mark the start, the direction the robot faces, how
many segments each leg is, and where the turns go. Then translate the sketch,
one leg per line:

```python
# (setup as above -- board, then board.wait_for_button())

drive_intersections(3)         # leg 1
turn_counterclockwise()        # corner
drive_intersections(1)         # leg 2
```

<TeacherNote title="Differentiation">

**More support:** give students a drawn path and have them write the matching
sequence. **More challenge:** a U-turn path (drive, turn right, drive one, turn
right, drive) — a good reminder that two right turns only reverse direction if you
drive a segment between them. Encourage planning on paper first; it prevents most
sequencing mistakes.

</TeacherNote>

## Wrap-up

- What's the basic shape of any grid path? (A sequence of drives and turns.)
- What has to come before a turn? (The arrival pair — the robot parked on the intersection.)
- What comes after a turn? (The next drive — a sensor is already on the new line.)
