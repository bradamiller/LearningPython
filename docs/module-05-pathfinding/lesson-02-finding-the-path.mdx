In Lesson 5-1 you labeled every intersection with its distance to the destination. That
tells you *how far* the start is, but not the path, and the robot needs a path: a list of
intersections to drive to, like the one the Manhattan planner hands it. So this lesson
answers two questions. What's the path? And how would we write a `compute_bfs_path()`
function that finds it? The lesson ends with a review of the whole algorithm in plain
words, which is what you'll turn into Python in Lesson 5-5.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Walk downhill from the start to find a shortest path, and explain why the path leaves the start out
- Explain why the wave starts at the destination
- Explain why a computer takes intersections oldest-first, and why the numbers are all it needs
- Carry out the algorithm exactly as a computer would, by hand, including on a grid with no path

</Objectives>

## Discuss · Where's the path?

Start from the grid you numbered at the end of Lesson 5-1: the wall at (1,0) and (1,1),
the destination D at (3,0) numbered 0, and the start S at (0,0), which got a 7.

1. You're standing on S, a 7. Which way do you go first?
2. Keep going. How do you choose each step, and when do you stop?
3. Somewhere you'll find two neighbors with the same number. Which one do you pick?
4. Why did we start the wave at D, and not at S?

<TeacherNote title="Leading the discussion (10 min)">

1. **Up, to (0,1), the 6.** It's the only neighbor with a smaller number. Let them notice
   that it goes *away* from D, and that the numbers say so with complete confidence.
2. **Always step to a neighbor one less; stop at 0, which is D.** Ask why it can't fail:
   every intersection got its number *from* a neighbor one less, so there is always a way
   down.
3. **Either one.** Both lead down to the 0 in the same number of steps. There can be
   several shortest paths; what's unique is the number of steps.
4. **So the walk starts where the robot is and goes forward to D.** The path comes out in
   driving order. Ask what would happen had we numbered from S: you would walk downhill
   from D back to S, and then have to turn the list around. A bonus worth saying: the
   numbers from D tell the way from *any* start, not just this one.

Their grid, for reference. One walk from the 7: up the left column, along the row y = 2,
down the right column.

```text
 3 |   6  5  4  3
 2 |   5  4  3  2
 1 |   6  X  2  1
 0 |   7  X  1  0
    -------------
       0  1  2  3
```

</TeacherNote>

## Walking downhill

Each step of the walk is an intersection the robot drives *to*. Written down in order,
they are the path. One answer for the wall grid is

```python
[(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2), (2, 2), (3, 2), (3, 1), (3, 0)]
```

The start is left out because the robot is already on it, as in Module 4's
`compute_manhattan_path`, so `len(path)` is the number of steps: 7. A classmate who picked
differently at a tie might have `[..., (2, 2), (2, 1), (2, 0), (3, 0)]`: also 7 steps,
also right. In Lesson 5-6 you'll test the planner by checking what must be true of any
correct answer, not by comparing against one list.

## Discuss · Teaching it to a computer

You numbered the grid with your eyes: you looked at the whole board and found "all the
2s" at once. A computer can't do that. It sees one intersection and its open neighbors,
the same limit your own rule had in Lesson 5-1.

1. A computer looks around **one intersection at a time**. In what order should it take
   them, so that every 1 is looked around before any 2?
2. How can it remember that order? What does it write down, and where does each newly
   numbered intersection go?
3. When is the wave finished?
4. To walk downhill, what does the computer need to know about each intersection? Does
   it need anything else?

<TeacherNote title="Leading the bridge (10–15 min): the most important ten minutes in the module">

This is where students usually get stuck: they can do it by hand, but can't see what a
program would *do*. Each answer below is one piece of the program, so write each on the
board as it's found, in words, and leave it up for the rest of the lesson.

1. **In the order they were numbered.** Every 1 was numbered before any 2, so taking
   them oldest-first deals with everything 1 step away before anything 2 steps away:
   the wave front moves out one step at a time, just as it did on paper.
2. **A list.** When an intersection gets its number, add it to the *end*. To choose
   where to look next, take the one at the *front*. Call it the **to-visit list**. Act it
   out if the room is flat: students in a line, the one at the front sits down and sends
   any unnumbered neighbors to the back.
3. **When the list is empty.** Then every intersection the wave can reach has its
   number. (On paper you could stop once S had a number; the computer just lets the wave
   finish, which on a 4×4 grid costs nothing.)
4. **Just its number.** That's all the walk uses: find a neighbor one less. Nothing else
   needs remembering. Say this plainly; students expect something more complicated, and
   it's why the code in Lesson 5-5 is short.

</TeacherNote>

## Review: the algorithm

Here is breadth-first search all in one place, the way you would explain it if someone
asked you how it works.

**Number the grid from the destination.** Write 0 on the destination and put it on a
to-visit list. Then, until the list is empty: take the intersection at the front of the
list, the one that has waited longest, and look at each of its open neighbors. A neighbor
that doesn't have a number yet gets one more than this intersection's number, and joins
the end of the list. A neighbor that already has a number is left alone. When the list is
empty, every intersection the wave could reach has its number: its steps to the
destination.

Because new intersections join the end and you always take from the front, everything 1
step from the destination is numbered before anything 2 steps away. That's the wave from
Lesson 5-1, moving out one step at a time.

**Walk downhill from the start.** If the start never got a number, the wave never reached
it, and there is no path. Otherwise, stand on the start and, until you're on the
destination, step to an open neighbor whose number is one less, writing down each
intersection you step onto. Leave the start off; the robot is already there. The
intersections you wrote down, in order, are the path the robot drives.

That's all it needs: one number per intersection. And while it numbers the grid it never
looks at where the start is, which is why walls in the way are no problem.

## Activity · Be the computer

Work in pairs. One of you is the computer: you may look only at the intersection you're
working on and its open neighbors, and you keep the to-visit list and the numbers on
paper. The other reads the review above aloud, one instruction at a time, and checks that
the computer does exactly that and nothing more.

Use a 3×3 grid with the whole middle column blocked: (1,0), (1,1) and (1,2). The start is
(0,0), the destination (2,0).

1. What ends the numbering, and which intersections got numbers?
2. Did the start get a number? What does that mean?
3. Swap jobs and run it once more, this time with only (1,1) blocked. What path do you get?

<Reveal title="Check your run" hint="run it first">

**Middle column blocked:** (2,0) gets 0. Its only open neighbor, (2,1), gets 1; then
(2,2) gets 2. Then the list is empty, so the numbering ends with only the right column
numbered. The start never got a number: the wave never reached it, so **there is no
path**. The program will hand back an empty list, `[]`.

**Only (1,1) blocked:** from (2,0), (1,0) and (2,1) get 1; (0,0) and (2,2) get 2; (0,1)
and (1,2) get 3; (0,2) gets 4. The start is a 2. Walk downhill: (1,0), a 1, then (2,0),
the 0. The path is `[(1, 0), (2, 0)]`.

</Reveal>

## Wrap-up

- How do you get the path from the numbers? (From the start, keep stepping to a neighbor one less until you reach 0.)
- Why does the wave start at the destination? (So the walk starts where the robot is and the path comes out in driving order.)
- Why does the computer take the oldest intersection first? (So the wave front moves out one step at a time.)
- What happens when the start is walled off? (The list empties without reaching it; there is no path.)

<TeacherNote title="Pacing, and why the review matters">

Two discussions and two activities: about 10, 15, 15 and 10 minutes. If time is short,
keep "Teaching it to a computer" and the review, and shorten the trace to steps 2 to 4.

Have students explain the review back to a partner in their own words before "Be the
computer". Lesson 5-3 goes through it asking what the program has to remember for each
part, and Lesson 5-5's skeleton lists its steps as numbered comments, with one line of
Python under each. A student who can explain the review can write the program; a student
who skipped it is translating a picture.

</TeacherNote>

**Formative:** a correct trace table; the "Be the computer" answers, especially that
