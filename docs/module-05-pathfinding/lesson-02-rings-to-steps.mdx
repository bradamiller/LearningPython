In Lesson 5-1 the class found the rings: every intersection numbered with its distance
from the start. The numbers say *how far* the destination is. Today you find the path
itself, and then work out how a computer would do the whole thing. By the end of the
lesson the method is written as a list of plain sentences, and in Lesson 5-5 each sentence
becomes one line of Python.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Walk back from the destination to find a shortest path, and explain why the path leaves the start out
- Explain why a computer takes intersections oldest-first, and why it writes down where each one came from
- Write breadth-first search as a numbered list of sentences a computer could follow
- Follow those sentences exactly, by hand, including on a grid with no path

</Objectives>

## Discuss · Where's the path?

Start from the grid you numbered at the end of Lesson 5-1: the wall at (1,0) and (1,1),
S at (0,0), and the destination D at (3,0), which got a 7.

1. Starting from S, can you tell which way to go first?
2. Try starting at D instead. How do you choose each step?
3. Somewhere you'll find two neighbors with the same number. Which one do you pick?

<TeacherNote title="Leading the discussion (8–10 min)">

1. **No.** S has only one open neighbor here, (0,1), but on most grids it has several 1s,
   and nothing on a 1 says whether it leads to D. Let a student try going forward from S
   on an open grid and watch them guess.
2. **Walk back downhill.** From the 7, step to any neighbor with a 6, then a 5, and so on
   to the 0. Ask why it can't fail: every intersection got its number *from* a neighbor one
   less, so there is always a way down.
3. **Either one.** Both lead down to the 0 in the same number of steps. There can be
   several shortest paths; what's unique is the number of steps.

Their grid, for reference, with one walk back from 7: up the left column, along the row
y = 2, down the right column.

```text
 3 |   3  4  5  6
 2 |   2  3  4  5
 1 |   1  X  5  6
 0 |   0  X  6  7
    -------------
       0  1  2  3
```

</TeacherNote>

## Walking back

The walk back gives the intersections from D to S. Written forward, *without* the start,
that is the path. One answer for the wall grid is

```python
[(0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2), (2, 2), (3, 2), (3, 1), (3, 0)]
```

Those are the intersections the robot drives *to*. The start is left out because the
robot is already on it, as in Module 4's `compute_manhattan_path`, so `len(path)` is the
number of steps: 7. A classmate who picked differently at a tie might have
`[..., (2, 2), (2, 1), (2, 0), (3, 0)]`: also 7 steps, also right. In Lesson 5-6 you'll
test the planner by checking what must be true of any correct answer, not by comparing
against one list.

## Discuss · Teaching it to a computer

You numbered the rings with your eyes: you looked at the whole board and found "all the
2s" at once. A computer can't do that. It sees one intersection and its open neighbors,
the same limit your own rule had in Lesson 5-1.

1. A computer looks around **one intersection at a time**. In what order should it take
   them, so that every 1 is looked around before any 2?
2. How can it remember that order? What does it write down, and where does each newly
   numbered intersection go?
3. On the walk back you searched for a smaller neighbor at every step. What could the
   computer write down at the moment it numbers an intersection, so it never has to
   search?
4. With that written down, does the computer still need the numbers at all?

<TeacherNote title="Leading the bridge (10–15 min): the most important ten minutes in the module">

This is where students usually get stuck: they can do it by hand, but can't see what a
program would *do*. Each answer below is one piece of the program, so write each on the
board as it's found, in words, and leave it up for the rest of the lesson.

1. **In the order they were numbered.** Every 1 was numbered before any 2, so taking
   them oldest-first finishes ring 1 before ring 2 starts.
2. **A list.** When an intersection gets its number, add it to the *end*. To choose
   where to look next, take the one at the *front*. Call it the **to-visit list**. Act it
   out if the room is flat: students in a line, the one at the front sits down and sends
   any unnumbered neighbors to the back.
3. **Where it came from**: the intersection being looked around when it was numbered.
   Then the walk back never searches; it just follows "came from" to the start.
4. **No.** The numbers did two jobs: "has it been reached yet?" and "which way back?".
   "Came from" does both: an intersection with a came-from entry has been reached, and it
   says which way back. The numbers were for *us*, to see why the first route found is
   shortest. Say this plainly; it surprises students, and it's why the code in Lesson
   5-5 has no numbers in it.

</TeacherNote>

## The method in sentences

Here is everything you just did, as sentences in order. Read them as a computer would:
do exactly what each one says and nothing else. In Lesson 5-5 **each sentence becomes one
line of Python**, so keep this list.

```text
 1. Write down that the start was reached, from nowhere.
 2. Put the start on the to-visit list.
 3. While the to-visit list is not empty:
 4.     Take the oldest one off the front. Call it current.
 5.     If current is the destination, stop looking.
 6.     For each open neighbor of current:
 7.         If that neighbor has not been reached yet:
 8.             Write down that it came from current.
 9.             Add it to the end of the to-visit list.
10. If the destination was never reached, there is no path.
11. Start with an empty path, standing on the destination.
12. Until you are back at the start:
13.     Add where you are standing to the path.
14.     Step back to where it came from.
15. Turn the path around. That is the answer.
```

Sentences 1 to 9 are the search; 10 to 15 are the walk back. The indents show what
repeats: sentences 4 to 9 happen again and again inside 3, and Python will indent them the
same way. Notice what isn't there: no ring numbers (sentence 7's "not reached yet" does
their job), and no looking at the destination except in sentence 5.

## Activity · Be the computer

Work in pairs. One of you reads the sentences aloud, one at a time; the other does
exactly what each says and keeps the to-visit list and the came-from notes on paper. No
looking at the whole grid: only the sentence being read.

Use a 3×3 grid with the whole middle column blocked: (1,0), (1,1) and (1,2). Start at
(0,0), destination (2,0).

1. Which sentence ends the search, and what is on the to-visit list when it does?
2. What does sentence 10 do?
3. Swap jobs and run it once more, this time with only (1,1) blocked. What path do you get?

<Reveal title="Check your run" hint="run the sentences first">

**Middle column blocked:** (0,0) is reached and taken. Its only open neighbor is (0,1),
which goes on the list. Then (0,1) adds (0,2). Then (0,2) has no neighbor that isn't
reached yet. The list is now empty, so sentence 3 ends the search, and (2,0) never got a
came-from note. Sentence 10 says **there is no path**; the program will hand back an
empty list, `[]`.

**Only (1,1) blocked:** (1,0) and (0,1) from (0,0). Then (1,0) adds (2,0), and (0,1)
adds (0,2). Next, (2,0) comes off the front and sentence 5 stops the search. Walking
back: (2,0) from (1,0) from (0,0). The path is `[(1, 0), (2, 0)]`.

</Reveal>

## Wrap-up

- How do you get the path from the ring numbers? (Walk back from the destination, always to a neighbor one less.)
- Why does the computer take the oldest intersection first? (So each ring is finished before the next.)
- Why doesn't the computer need the numbers? (A came-from note says both "reached" and "which way back".)
- What happens when the destination is walled off? (The to-visit list empties first; there is no path.)

<TeacherNote title="Pacing, and why the sentences matter">

Two discussions and two activities: about 10, 15, 15 and 10 minutes. If time is short,
keep "Teaching it to a computer" and "The method in sentences" and shorten the trace to
steps 2 to 4.

Post the fifteen sentences on the wall for the rest of the module. Lesson 5-3 asks of
each one what the program has to remember to do it; Lesson 5-5 pastes them into
`robot.py` as numbered comments, and students write one line of Python under each. A
student who can point at a sentence and say what it does can write the program; a
student who skipped the sentences is translating a picture.

</TeacherNote>
