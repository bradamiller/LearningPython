Your Manhattan robot has no way around a **blocked** intersection. Today you find a way
around one by eye, then work out, as a class, a method that never guesses. It's called
**breadth-first search**. No code today, just paper.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain why the Manhattan algorithm can't handle a blocked intersection
- Explain why "look at the grid and guess" isn't a method a computer can use
- Number every intersection with its distance from the start, one ring at a time, around blocked intersections
- Explain why the first number written on the destination is the shortest path length, so you can stop there

</Objectives>

## Why Manhattan isn't enough

When your usual route to school is closed, your GPS finds a detour. The robot needs to do
the same. The Manhattan algorithm from Module 4 knows one strategy, across first, then up
or down. It has no concept of an obstacle, so it drives right into a blocked intersection.

A maps app solves the same puzzle: a closed road is blocked, and heavy traffic makes some
streets slower. This module and the next build the ideas those apps use:

- **This module, breadth-first search**, finds a route around blocked intersections.
- **Module 6, Dijkstra's algorithm**, finds the *fastest* route when some streets are
  slow. Real apps use faster descendants of it (A\* is the best known), but the core
  idea is the one you'll write.

## Activity · Finding a path around

On a 3×3 grid, block **(1,0)** and find the shortest path from (0,0) to (2,0). A blocked
intersection is out of play: the robot can't stop on it or drive through it.

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-m5-01/detour-xy.svg"
  alt="A 3 by 3 grid with (1, 0) blocked and its lines removed. S is (0, 0) and D is (2, 0). A red dashed arrow shows Manhattan trying to go straight across to the right through (1, 0), which is blocked."
  caption="Straight across is closed. Find the way around before you open the answer."
/>

Manhattan's straight line goes through (1,0), so it fails. Trace a way around with your
finger and count the steps.

<Reveal title="Check your route" hint="find one by hand first">

One shortest detour is (0,0) → (0,1) → (1,1) → (2,1) → (2,0): **4 steps**. Any other
4-step route around (1,0) is just as correct; there's usually more than one shortest path.

</Reveal>

Without the obstacle the path is 2 steps: the steps across plus the steps up or down.
That count is the **Manhattan distance**. **Obstacles make paths longer**, so the
Manhattan distance is only a lower bound.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="With (1,0) blocked, the shortest path from (0,0) to (2,0) is 4 steps, but the Manhattan distance is 2. What does that tell you?"
  options={[
    {text: 'The Manhattan distance is wrong'},
    {text: 'With obstacles, the real shortest path can be longer than the Manhattan distance', correct: true},
    {text: 'Obstacles always add exactly 2 steps'},
    {text: 'The destination is unreachable'},
  ]}
  explanation="Manhattan distance is the shortest path with NO obstacles. Blocked intersections can force a longer detour, so it becomes a lower bound."
/>

## Activity · Explain your method to a computer

You found that path by looking at the whole grid. A computer can't: it only sees one
intersection and which of its neighbors are open. Write your *rule*, in numbered steps,
for someone who can only see that much. Your rule must get them to the destination by a
shortest path, every time, on any grid.

Give it two minutes, then test your rule on this one:

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-m5-03/wall-problem-xy.svg"
  alt="A 4 by 4 grid. S is the bottom-left intersection, (0, 0); D is (3, 0), straight across the bottom row, y = 0. Intersections (1, 0) and (1, 1) are blocked and crossed out, with their lines removed."
  caption="Your rule can only look at one intersection and its open neighbors at a time."
/>

Start at `S` (0,0), destination `D` (3,0), straight across the bottom row, with a wall at
(1,0) and (1,1).

<TeacherNote title="What you're fishing for">

Nearly every rule students write is Manhattan with a patch: "head toward the goal;
if it's blocked, go around." Let them apply it to the grid above. "Go around" means
stepping up to (0,1), which is still blocked on its right, so they go up to (0,2), and now
the rule says "head toward the goal" — right to (1,2) — still *away* from the bottom row. Some
rules loop here; most can't say how far "around" to go. The point isn't to make
them fail, it's for them to feel that a rule which *looks at the goal* can't handle
a wall that forces you away from it. The discussion that follows builds a method that
never looks at the goal at all.

</TeacherNote>

## Discuss · How far away is everything?

Draw a 4×4 grid and mark S in the bottom-left corner, (0,0). No obstacles yet. Answer
each question before you go on to the next.

1. On our grid, what does "shortest" mean?
2. Forget the destination for a moment. Which intersections can the robot reach in
   exactly **1** step? Write a 1 on each.
3. Which can it reach in exactly **2** steps? Write a 2 on each. How did you find them?
4. Keep going until every intersection has a number. Say the destination is the far
   corner, (3,3). When its number is written, could there still be a shorter way to it
   that you missed?
5. Now put back the wall from the last activity, (1,0) and (1,1) blocked, and number
   the grid again from S. Do you need a new rule for the blocked intersections?

<TeacherNote title="Leading the discussion (15–20 min)">

Draw the grid big on the board and write the numbers there as the class gives them. The
goal is that *they* say the rule; your job is to ask the next question.

1. **Fewest blocks driven.** Every block is one step. If anyone says "the straightest",
   ask: "Is a path with 5 steps ever shorter than one with 4?"
2. **(1,0) and (0,1).** Easy on purpose. The real move is that the question is about
   distance *from S*, not about getting to D. Don't mention D again until question 4.
3. **(2,0), (1,1) and (0,2).** Someone will say S, by going out and back. Ask: "Do we learn
   anything by writing a 2 on S? We already know it's 0." That is the first discovery:
   **only number an intersection that has no number yet.** Then press on "How did you
   find them?" until someone says *you looked at the neighbors of the 1s*. Generalize out
   loud and write it on the board: **"Every unnumbered neighbor of a 2 gets a 3."** That
   sentence is the whole algorithm; underline it.
4. **(3,3) gets a 6, and no, nothing was missed.** Let them argue. The convincing reason:
   every intersection 2 steps away was numbered before any 3 was written, so if (3,3)
   could be reached in fewer steps, it would already have a smaller number. So the first
   number written on the destination is the shortest distance, and **you can stop the
   moment the destination gets a number.**
5. **No new rule.** A blocked intersection just never gets a number, and the numbers flow
   around it. (3,0) gets a 7. Point at the first steps, which go up, *away* from (3,0):
   "This method never looked at the destination. Is that a weakness or a strength?" It's
   why it works where the head-toward-the-goal rules from the last activity didn't. Keep
   this numbered grid on the board; Lesson 5-2 starts from it.

Their grid for question 5:

```text
 3 |   3  4  5  6
 2 |   2  3  4  5
 1 |   1  X  5  6
 0 |   0  X  6  7
    -------------
       0  1  2  3
```

</TeacherNote>

## What you found: ripples

Here is the method the class just worked out:

1. Write **0** on the start.
2. Every intersection one step away gets a **1**.
3. Every intersection one step from a 1 that *doesn't have a number yet* gets a **2**.
4. Keep going, finishing each ring before you start the next, until the destination gets
   a number.

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-m5-03/bfs-rings-xy.svg"
  alt="Left: a 3 by 3 grid with the start numbered 0 at the bottom-left, its neighbors 1, the next corners 2, the next two 3, and the top-right destination 4; the center intersection is crossed out with no number. Right: the same grid with a small arrow on each intersection pointing back to the one it was reached from, and the walk-back from 4 down the right side and along the bottom highlighted in red."
  caption="Ripples spreading out from the start, around a blocked intersection. Each ring is exactly one step further out. The arrows on the right are next lesson's idea: they turn the numbers into a path."
/>

The destination's number is its distance from the start, in steps, and the first number
written on it is the smallest it could ever get: every shorter distance was finished
first. That's why it's called **breadth-first search**: it goes wide (the whole ring)
before it goes deep. It never looks at the destination, which is why a wall that forces
you away from it is no problem.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="An intersection already has a 2 on it. Later, while looking around an intersection with a 3, you find it's a neighbor. What do you write on it?"
  options={[
    {text: 'Nothing — it keeps its 2', correct: true},
    {text: '4, because 3 + 1'},
    {text: '3, the smaller of 3 and 4'},
    {text: 'Cross out the 2 and write 4'},
  ]}
  explanation="An intersection is numbered once, the first time it's reached. Because rings are finished in order, that first number is already the smallest possible."
/>

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="The destination has just been given a 5. Should you keep numbering, in case a shorter way turns up?"
  options={[
    {text: 'No: every intersection 4 or fewer steps away is already numbered, so 5 is the shortest', correct: true},
    {text: 'Yes: finish the whole grid, then take the smallest number the destination got'},
    {text: 'Yes: a path that heads toward the destination might be shorter'},
    {text: 'It depends on how many intersections are blocked'},
  ]}
  explanation="Rings are finished in order, so the first number written on the destination can't be beaten. You can stop right there."
/>

## The ripple on a bigger grid

A 3×3 is small enough to trace by hand, but too small to show the *shape* of
breadth-first search. Here is the same rule on an 8 by 6 grid with a wall across the
middle.

<PlayFigure
  src="/img/lesson-m5-03/fanout-bfs.svg"
  alt="An animation of breadth-first search on a grid of 8 by 6 intersections with a wall of four blocked intersections at x = 3, from y = 1 to y = 4. S is (0, 4) and D is (7, 4), on the other side of the wall. Each intersection lights up with its step count from S when the search first reaches it, so the numbers spread out from S in even rings. The ripple flows around both ends of the wall. When D is taken off the list the search stops, and the walk-back from D is drawn in red: up, along the top past the wall, and down to D, 9 steps."
  caption="Same rule as your grid: every intersection one step out is numbered before any two steps out. On a bigger grid that rule spreads like a ripple in a pond, and bends around whatever is in the way." />

The search spreads evenly, even *away* from D: it has no idea where D is until it gets
there. It stops as soon as D comes off the list, so a few far corners never get a number.

## Wrap-up

- Why can't Manhattan handle a blocked intersection? (One fixed route, no idea of obstacles.)
- What does the number on an intersection mean? (Its distance from the start, in steps.)
- Do blocked intersections need a special rule? (No: they never get a number, and the rings flow around them.)
- Why can you stop as soon as the destination gets a number? (Everything closer was numbered first.)

<TeacherNote title="Make Manhattan's failure vivid, then let them find the rule">

This lesson is the *why* for the whole module. Spend real time on the hook: show a blocked
intersection sitting on Manhattan's route and let students feel that the old algorithm is
stuck. Draw big grids on the board; have students come up, cross out blocked
intersections, and trace detours with markers. Let them struggle to state a rule; that
struggle is what makes the ripples worth having.

The one habit that matters is **finish the ring before starting the next**. Resist
introducing a `distances` table or "infinity" here. BFS doesn't need them, and they're
exactly what Module 6 adds for a reason. If a student asks about slow roads, "good
question, Module 6."

Don't bring in the to-visit list today. Students number a whole ring at once, by eye,
and that's right for now: Lesson 5-2's bridge to the computer starts from noticing that a
computer can't do that.

Words like *graph*, *node* and *dictionary* wait until Lesson 5-3, when the program needs
them. "Intersection" and "neighbor" are enough today.

</TeacherNote>

<TeacherNote title="Assessment & next lesson">
