**Robots.** One XRP per two students works; one each is better from Module 2 on,
where line tracking is fiddly and students need their own runs. A low battery
presents as a dozen unrelated-looking bugs, and it kills Bluetooth before it kills
anything else — which is why the battery system below is worth setting up
properly.

**Computers.** Chrome or Edge, and nothing to install: the whole course runs in
XRP Code in the browser, Blockly first and then Python files in the same editor.
Safari and Firefox load the page but can't connect to a robot.

**USB cables.** One per robot, and they must be **data** cables — charge-only
cables are the single most common first-day failure. Every robot also needs one
USB connection before it can go wireless, so plan that pass before day one.

**Floor space.** Open floor for Modules 1 and 2, with a taped line course for the
line-tracking work. Modules 3 through 5 need a taped grid, which is worth laying
down once and leaving — three modules use it.

**Whiteboard panels.** Hardware stores — Lowe's, Home Depot — sell large
whiteboard-faced panels cheaply, and will usually cut them down for you. They do
two jobs. They give you a driving surface you can put on the floor *or* lay across
a couple of tables, which matters when floor space is short or you'd rather
students work standing up. And they're what the robot draws on: with a marker
fitted, the shape lessons in Module 1 stop being abstract and leave a square, a
triangle or a hexagon behind on the board. *(Specific panel recommendations to
come.)*

**Dry-erase markers.** They fit the marker holder on the robot, which is how a
drawn shape becomes the evidence that the program worked — a square that doesn't
close tells you more than any error message. Keep spares; they dry out, and a
faint line is hard to judge. You'll want them for drawing courses and grids on the
panels too.

**Tape.** For line courses on the floor, matte black electrical or gaffer tape on
a light surface gives the reflectance sensor the contrast it needs. Glossy tape
reflects and reads light.

**Rechargeable AA batteries and chargers.** Buy more batteries than you have
robots, and enough chargers to keep up. The system that works in a classroom is
two labeled boxes — **charged** and **needs charging** — sitting next to a bank of
chargers. Students take a charged set at the start of class and drop the spent
cells into a charger or the needs-charging box on the way out. Nobody waits for a
robot to charge, and a robot that dies mid-period is a thirty-second swap instead
of a lost lesson. Somebody does have to move cells from the box into the chargers,
so make it a student job with a name on it.

**A place to keep them.** Robots survive better in numbered bins than in a pile,
and numbering pays off again when a room full of XRPs shows up in the Bluetooth
list — label each robot with its ID the first time you connect it.
