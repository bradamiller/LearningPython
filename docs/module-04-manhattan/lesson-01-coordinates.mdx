To tell the robot *where to go*, you need a way to name every spot on the grid. This
lesson builds that system — **(x, y) coordinates** — with paper and the
physical grid, no code yet. A solid mental model here makes tuples, lists, and the
Manhattan algorithm click later.

<Objectives>

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Explain what a coordinate system is and why a robot needs one
- Name any intersection with an `(x, y)` pair
- Tell x (across) from y (away from you), and remember order matters
- Count the steps between two positions (the Manhattan distance)

</Objectives>

## Two numbers name any spot

You already know coordinate systems: Battleship ("B4"), chess ("e5"), a spreadsheet
cell ("A1"), GPS latitude and longitude. Two numbers are enough to pinpoint any spot
on a flat surface. Our grid uses **(x, y)**, the same order as a graph in math class,
with `(0, 0)` at the **bottom-left corner — the one nearest you** as the grid lies in
front of you:

- **x** (first number) is how far *across to the right* from the left edge — the left column is x = `0`.
- **y** (second number) is how far *away from you* from the near edge — the bottom row, nearest you, is y = `0`.

North is away from you, up the page. So x counts east and y counts north.

Two things to burn in: we **count from zero** (the bottom-left is `(0, 0)`, not
`(1, 1)`), and **order matters** — `(2, 3)` and `(3, 2)` are *different* spots.

## Counting steps: Manhattan distance

Since the robot follows lines (no diagonals), the distance between two spots is just
the steps across plus the steps up or down:

> steps = (x difference) + (y difference)

From `(0, 0)` to `(2, 3)`: 2 right + 3 up = **5 steps**. This "blocks
you'd walk" total is called the **Manhattan distance** — named for walking a city
grid where you can't cut through buildings.

<KnowledgeCheck
  question="How many steps from (0, 0) to (3, 2), moving only along grid lines?"
  options={[
    {text: '5', correct: true},
    {text: '6'},
    {text: '1'},
    {text: '3'},
  ]}
  explanation="3 right + 2 up = 5 steps. That sum is the Manhattan distance."
/>

<TeacherNote title="x/y order is the #1 stumble">

Repeat the convention constantly — "x first, y second; x is across, y is away from
you" — and consider posting it. Students who have graphed in math class know the
order already; what is new is counting from zero and that `(0, 0)` is the corner
nearest them. Zero-indexing trips most of them: they count from 1 by habit, so name
*why* (Python indexes from 0, and matching now avoids pain later). If you can, have
students stand at the near edge, physically **walk the grid** and call out their
coordinates — kinesthetic beats diagrams here. Keep it code-free; when they ask "when
do we code?", answer "next lesson — today you're building the map the code will use."

</TeacherNote>

<TeacherNote title="Differentiation">

**More support:** start on a 3×3 grid; color-code each column; hand out a

intersections on an N×N grid?" (N×N); and the combinatorics teaser — how many
*different* shortest paths exist from `(0,0)` to `(2,3)`? Note there are many equal-
length paths; the Manhattan algorithm will pick one specific route (across first, then
up or down).

## Wrap-up

- Which number comes first, and what does it mean? (x — how far across to the right.)
- Why start counting at 0? (To match how Python indexes, avoiding confusion later.)
- How do you find the number of steps between two spots? (x difference + y difference.)
