## How we compute the route

The robot plans every route the same simple way:

> **Go across until x is right. Then go up or down until y is right.**

That's the whole algorithm. It always gives an L-shaped route (or a straight line, if
the robot is already in the right column or row), and it never takes a step it
doesn't need.

It's called the **Manhattan algorithm**, after a real place. Most of Manhattan, the
island in the middle of New York City, is laid out as a rectangular grid. Long
**avenues** run north and south up the island, shorter numbered **streets** cross them
east and west, and every rectangle in between is a city block packed solid with
buildings.

A taxi can't drive diagonally from one corner to another, because there are buildings
in the way. It goes along a street, then up an avenue, and the trip is simply *blocks
across + blocks up or down*. New Yorkers even give directions that way: "two blocks
east, then three up." Your robot is in exactly the same situation, because it can only
drive along the tape. Lesson 4-4 builds the algorithm properly.

<Callout kind="note" title="The big assumption: every street is clear">

The Manhattan algorithm assumes nothing is in the way: every line of tape and every
intersection on the grid can be driven. That's why it can be so simple. It never has
to look for a detour, because it assumes there's never a reason to take one.

Real streets aren't always clear. **Module 5** puts obstacles on the grid and teaches
the robot to plan around them, and even to notice a new one while it's driving.
**Module 6** (optional) handles streets that are open but slow, where the shortest
route in steps isn't the fastest.

</Callout>

<TeacherNote title="Manhattan, for the curious">

The grid dates from the Commissioners' Plan of 1811, which laid streets and avenues
over most of the island before much of it was built on. Two details students enjoy:
the grid isn't aligned with true north (it is turned roughly 29 degrees, so
"Manhattan north" is a local convention), and the same distance is called **taxicab
distance** in mathematics. Neither matters for the robot; the map is a hook, not a
lesson. Lesson 4-4 opens with "Why Manhattan?" and assumes students have seen this.

</TeacherNote>

## The whole program — really

Here's the *entire* main program you're building toward. Seven lines:

```python
position = (0, 0)
heading = 0                      # facing North
destinations = [(2, 0), (2, 3), (0, 3)]

for dest in destinations:
    path = compute_manhattan_path(position, dest)
    print("Path:", path)
    position, heading = drive_path(path, position, heading)
```

In plain English: *start at corner (0, 0) facing North; for each destination, compute
the path from where we are, print it, drive it, and remember where we ended up.* You
don't need to understand `compute_manhattan_path` or `drive_path` yet — just see that
the program is short **because the functions do all the work**.

(Those seven lines are the *interesting* part. In your `robot.py` they are the body of
the module's last test function, and the two functions they call live in two new
sections above it — MANHATTAN PLANNER and NAVIGATOR — on top of the LINE TRACKING and
GRID DRIVING sections you already have. The `RUN` block waits for the button as always.
All of that is left out here so the shape stands out.)

## Two halves, two jobs

<CardGrid>
  <InfoCard tag="Planner" title="compute_manhattan_path">
    Told where the robot is and where it wants to go, it computes a <strong>list of intersections</strong> to follow. It never touches the robot.
  </InfoCard>
  <InfoCard tag="Driver" title="drive_path">
    Given that list, it <strong>drives the robot</strong> through each intersection — turning and line-following — and reports where it ended up. It never decides the route.
  </InfoCard>
</CardGrid>

<Figure
  src="/img/lesson-m4-00/two-halves.svg"
  alt="Two boxes. The planner, compute_manhattan_path(), takes a position and destination, plans the route and never drives. It hands a path, such as [(1,0), (2,0)], to the driver, drive_path(), which drives the route and never plans. The driver hands back position and heading, where the robot ended up, which go into the next plan."
  caption="A path goes one way, from planner to driver; where the robot ended up comes back for the next plan."
/>

The planner produces a path; the driver consumes it. Each half has exactly one
responsibility — that's called **separation of concerns**, and it makes bugs easy to
find: a wrong route is a planner problem; a wrong turn is a driver problem. And because
the planner is *told* where the robot is rather than remembering, the printed path is
always checkable before the robot moves.

## Breaking a big problem into parts

Where did the two halves come from? Nobody writes "drive to every destination, all by
itself" as one big function and gets it right. It's too much to hold in your head at
once, and when it goes wrong you'd have no idea where to look. So we split it.
Breaking a big problem into smaller parts you can build one at a time is called
**problem decomposition** (you'll also hear **top-down design**: start from the whole
goal and work down to the pieces). It's how almost every program longer than a page
gets written.

It's a method, not a lucky guess, and it has four steps you can use on any project:

1. **State the whole problem in one sentence.** If you can't, you don't know yet what you're building.
2. **Name the parts, and say what each one takes in and hands back.** This is the step that matters most, because it's how the parts will fit together later.
3. **Build and test each part on its own.** A part you've tested alone is one you can trust when something goes wrong.
4. **Connect them.** If steps 2 and 3 were done well, this is the short one.

Here is the method applied to this module:

| Step | In Module 4 |
|---|---|
| 1 · The whole problem | Visit each destination in order, on its own. |
| 2 · The parts | **Planner:** takes where the robot is and where it's going; hands back a list of intersections. **Driver:** takes that list, plus where the robot is and which way it faces; hands back where it ended up and which way it faces. |
| 3 · Build and test each | The planner in Lessons 4-4 to 4-6, on screen, with the robot sitting still. The driver in Lessons 4-7 and 4-8, one leg at a time. |
| 4 · Connect them | The plan, print, drive test in Lesson 4-8, then the seven lines in Lesson 4-9. |

The planner, compute_manhattan_path(), built in Lessons 4-4 to 4-6, is small enough to build and test in one go. An arrow from planner to driver is labeled list of intersections, the one thing they share."
  caption="The module's problem, broken down. The planner is small enough to build in one go; the driver gets split once more."
/>

**Parts can have parts.** If a part is still too big to write and test in one
sitting, split it again with the same four steps. The driver is: one small function
works out which way to face, one turns until the robot faces that way, and your
Module 2 line-tracking functions do the actual driving. You can stop splitting when
you can say what a piece does in one sentence and test it on its own.

**What passes between the parts is the agreement.** The only thing the planner hands
the driver is a list of intersections. Because that was decided in step 2, before
either half was written, each half can be built without the other, and either one can
be replaced later. Module 5 does exactly that: a new planner that steers around
obstacles hands the same kind of list to the same driver, and the driver doesn't
change at all.

## Your roadmap

Every lesson in this module builds one piece of that program:

- **Lesson 4-1** — coordinates: the `(x, y)` system
- **Lesson 4-2** — tuples: storing a position like `(2, 3)` (scratch work, nothing goes in the file yet)
- **Lesson 4-3** — lists: storing a path like `[(1,0), (2,0), (3,0)]` (scratch work too)
- **Lesson 4-4** — the Manhattan algorithm: computing paths on paper, then in code — the MANHATTAN PLANNER section of `robot.py` opens
- **Lesson 4-5** — the planner function: its name, its promises, and why it is told where the robot is
- **Lesson 4-6** — testing your code without a robot: a test harness joins the planner section
- **Lesson 4-7** — turning logic: headings as numbers — the NAVIGATOR section opens
- **Lesson 4-8** — driving the path, built on your Module 2 line-tracking functions
- **Lesson 4-9** — the final project: it all comes together in one test function

Notice `drive_path` reuses the Module 2 functions for the actual driving — you're not
starting from scratch. That's the payoff of building a toolkit. (If you did the Module 2
class extensions, Lessons 4-5 and 4-8 each have an optional class version too.)
